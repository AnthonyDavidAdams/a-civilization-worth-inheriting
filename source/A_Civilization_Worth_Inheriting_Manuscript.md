## The Thousand Year Old Book That Helped the Future Change Its Mind

**Anthony David Adams imagined a civilization that could thrive without deciding, once and for all, what thriving had to mean. His readers are still arguing about it.**

*By Leila Okafor*

*ERIE BASIN, September 25, 3026*

The meeting was supposed to last twenty minutes. A neighborhood wanted to widen a footbridge. The watershed's representative objected to the proposed pilings. An artificial person responsible for maintaining the bridge said the community had budgeted for construction but not for replacing its joints. Two children wanted somewhere to jump into the water.

By the time the participants agreed to commission a different design, ninety minutes had passed.

“There are days,” said the chair, Mara Ilyan, “when you wish nobody had ever read that book.”

The book was *A Civilization Worth Inheriting*, published a thousand years ago by Anthony David Adams. Its anniversary has brought the familiar commemorations: new editions, disputed translations, an exhibition of early handwritten annotations. It has also renewed a more interesting question. How much credit can a book claim for a world built by people who largely stopped reading it?

Most residents of the Erie Basin encounter its ideas through ordinary obligations. A building needs a maintenance endowment. A consequential automated decision can be appealed. A proposal affecting the watershed must answer someone empowered to speak for its living systems. Public institutions periodically have to justify their continued existence.

Few residents know which of these practices appeared in Adams's original 108 patterns, which developed elsewhere, and which were added by later contributors. Scholars disagree about the distinctions, too.

“You cannot explain a millennium through a single author,” said Amara Velez, a historian at the Atlantic University of Public Memory. “What you can trace is a vocabulary. People in very different places began asking the same awkward questions.”

Among them: Who bears the costs? Who is absent from the decision? Can the people who inherit this arrangement change it?

Adams wrote during a period when institutions could move money across the planet almost instantly but struggled to maintain soil, care for children, or agree on the obligations attached to increasingly capable machines. Much public debate treated the future as a contest between catastrophe and technological rescue.

His book approached civilization through recurring design problems. How should a community care for someone who cannot work? How should a river cross political borders? What powers should the owner of an artificial intelligence possess over people who depend on it? What would authorize any one group to speak for Earth?

The form acknowledged an older influence: *A Pattern Language*, the 1977 work by Christopher Alexander and his collaborators, which organized architectural problems and possible solutions into an interconnected language. Adams extended that approach to the conditions of collective life. A shared meal and a treaty concerning powerful machines belonged in the same book because both influenced whether strangers could become trustworthy neighbors.

Its early reception, according to documents assembled for the anniversary, was uneven. Admirers circulated the patterns as workshop materials. Critics found the project implausibly broad. Some pointed out that traditions of stewardship, commons management, and responsibility to descendants had existed long before a Western author organized them into a numbered list.

Later editions addressed that criticism by identifying particular contributors and traditions, documenting disagreements, and allowing communities to refuse incorporation into the project. The resulting book became considerably less tidy.

Its practical influence grew through contracts and budgets. Several municipalities experimented with the pattern “Pay for Maintenance First,” requiring new infrastructure to include a credible plan for its upkeep. Others tested “A Seat for the Absent,” funding independent advocates for people and living systems whose interests otherwise disappeared from deliberation. Implementation reports traveled alongside the patterns. So did records of failure.

The failures mattered. In the twenty-third century, several governments used appeals to future generations to justify forced relocation. Their officials quoted Adams. So did the residents resisting them, invoking passages that forbade sacrificing living people to an administrator's preferred future.

The episode produced lasting restrictions on the authority of guardians appointed to represent descendants. They could require disclosure, bring challenges, and force review. They could not rule indefinitely on behalf of people who did not yet exist.

Animal advocates have been similarly unsentimental about the book's legacy. Early applications counted hectares of habitat while overlooking what happened to individual animals inside them. Later versions required separate consideration of ecological integrity and suffering. The two concerns still conflict, particularly in disputes over intervention in wild ecosystems.

“A forest can be recovering while an animal is in agony,” said Neri Sol, a comparative welfare researcher. “The language eventually learned to hold both facts.”

Artificial persons challenged another weakness. Early editions distinguished machine capability from possible experience but offered little agreement on how to assess the latter. Later contributors developed separate procedures for regulating dangerous capabilities and considering claims to moral and legal standing. Current protections remain differentiated and contested; their authors rejected both automatic personhood for fluent software and the assumption that ownership settled the matter.

The book's most unexpected afterlife arrived after the independently verified signal of 2684. The evidence supported an engineered extraterrestrial source. Its meaning was uncertain. Governments, scientific institutions, commercial networks, and religious communities each claimed some authority over a response.

Participants in the eventual contact assembly cited “No One Speaks for Earth” to challenge unilateral transmission. They also cited “The Right to Remain Unknown” in arguing that a reply should not disclose information about communities that had withheld consent.

The assembly took seventy-one years to authorize a limited response. Whether that delay represented prudence or paralysis remains a popular subject of historical argument. Given the distance, nobody involved could expect a prompt answer. In the meantime, the work of comparing possible interpretations produced new mathematics and an enduring collaboration among human and artificial researchers.

The anniversary arrives in a world that has survived without solving every problem Adams identified. There are disputes over habitat, resentment toward inherited wealth, and communities whose freedom exists more convincingly in their constitutions than in daily life. Some coastal homelands survive only in archives and the practices of their displaced descendants. Much of what was lost in the early centuries has never returned.

Still, the wider recovery is visible: waters people can enter, cities where care is easier to obtain, institutions that have learned to surrender powers, and flourishing societies beyond Earth that remain dependent on obligations of their own making.

For Velez, the book's importance lies in how its readers handled its inadequacy.

“A text that lasts this long usually acquires people whose job is to defend it,” she said. “This one also acquired people whose job was to replace parts of it.”

Back at the footbridge, the revised brief reserved room for the stream, specified replaceable joints, and sent the children's request to a swimming-safety review. The project was still over budget.

Ilyan closed the meeting and asked for two volunteers to inspect the old bridge the following morning.

For all the attention to the next thousand years, someone still had to go down and look.

# Book description

## A Civilization Worth Inheriting

*A Pattern Language for the Next Thousand Years*

*By Anthony David Adams*

**What would we build today if we wanted life to be flourishing a thousand years from now?**

A child needs somewhere safe to play. A river needs room to move. A powerful artificial intelligence needs limits that survive changes in its ownership. A civilization needs ways to remember what it has learned—and to recognize when those lessons have become mistakes.

In *A Civilization Worth Inheriting*, Anthony David Adams brings these questions into one practical, expansive work.

Inspired by the interconnected design approach of *A Pattern Language*, this book presents **108 patterns for a thriving civilization**. Each addresses a recurring problem, proposes a usable response, explores its tradeoffs, and connects to the other patterns it needs to succeed.

Begin with a household, a company, a neighborhood, or a watershed. Follow the connections outward to planetary institutions, artificial minds, settlements beyond Earth, and the possibility of contact with extraterrestrial intelligence.

Inside, you will explore how to:

- Make care, belonging, beauty, and meaningful work part of everyday life.

- Give animals and ecosystems standing in decisions that shape their futures.

- Build economies that reward repair and share the gains of automation.

- Govern powerful technologies while preserving freedom, accountability, and room for discovery.

- Prepare for catastrophe without organizing society around permanent emergency.

- Approach other worlds and unfamiliar intelligences with curiosity, restraint, and consent.

- Leave descendants institutions they can understand, maintain, challenge, and change.

The patterns range from **A Common Table** and **Pay for Maintenance First** to **Freedom From Machine Persuasion** and **No One Speaks for Earth**. They are proposals to test and improve, with explicit attention to where evidence ends and ethical judgment begins.

Written for founders, citizens, designers, educators, and anyone ready to take responsibility for a piece of the world, this book offers places to begin at a human scale.

**The next thousand years will be shaped by decisions already being made. We can make more of them worth inheriting.**

# How to use this language

A civilization is made partly from arrangements so familiar that they disappear from view: who cares for a child, who may cross a piece of land, who can question a decision, who maintains the water system, and who inherits the consequences when the people responsible have gone.

This book asks how those arrangements might support flourishing over a thousand years and beyond. The horizon is an invitation to enlarge responsibility. It is not a forecast of what the world will look like, a demand to preserve our present institutions forever, or a reason to postpone the needs of people alive now.

The word civilization is used here for the overlapping systems through which lives are sustained, knowledge is carried, power is organized, and meaning is made. It does not imply a single culture, a hierarchy of peoples, or one destination toward which everyone must move. A thriving future could contain many civilizations and many forms of life within them.

## A pattern is a proposal in context

Each pattern names a recurring problem and proposes a response. Its usefulness depends on circumstances and on other patterns. Local autonomy needs obligations to neighbors. Innovation needs a fair distribution of benefits. Preservation needs room for change. Care needs consent, resources, and the freedom of both the person receiving it and the person providing it.

The opening chapter develops the book's central argument at greater length. The remaining chapters are deliberately compact so that they can be read together, compared, and carried into a working conversation. Each provides a proposed response, a practical form, ways it can fail, a first move, and connections to other chapters.

The form is indebted to Christopher Alexander and his collaborators' *A Pattern Language*. The proposals here address a different and wider field of questions. Their appearance in a pattern language does not make them established laws. [S01]

## Three ways to read

Read from beginning to end to follow the movement from responsibility and ordinary life to planetary stewardship, artificial minds, continuity, possible cosmic neighbors, and practical application.

Read from a problem when something already needs attention. Use the contents and entry-point indexes to find several relevant patterns. Follow their connections until the important tensions become visible.

Read with a place by beginning at Pattern 100. Gather the people involved, choose a tractable question, and use a small set of patterns to develop and examine a response. The language becomes more useful when it meets a real constraint, a budget, a disagreement, or an unexpected result.

## What kind of claim is being made?

The book combines ethical proposals, institutional designs, references to research, and speculative scenarios. These have different kinds of support. A scientific finding can inform a decision without settling whose interests should count or how burdens should be shared.

Named sources are identified with codes such as [S05] and listed in the source guide. The worked situations are illustrative scenarios, not reported case studies. Chapters about future settlements, unfamiliar artificial minds, and extraterrestrial contact explore possibilities; they do not predict that those possibilities will occur.

Treat confident language in a pattern's proposal as an invitation to examine a design principle. Ask what would make it wrong, which conditions it assumes, and whose experience could correct it. The most useful reader may be the one who finds a better formulation.

## Keep a record and return

For any application, record the place, the version of the pattern, the people involved, the intended benefit, the burdens, the uncertainty, and the review date. A short honest record is more valuable than an elaborate declaration nobody will revisit.

The companion research and interview practices at the end of this book are designed to support that work. They make room for evidence, experience, and disagreement to accumulate around each pattern while preserving the ability to change the pattern itself.

Begin wherever you have a real relationship and some capacity to help. The whole civilization is too large to hold in one pair of hands. A useful responsibility is not.


# Part I  Responsibility across time

*Establish what a civilization owes to life now and to those who will inherit its decisions.*

# 1 Leave Room for the Future

**Use this pattern whenever a decision will outlast the people making it, or impose consequences on lives they do not control.**

At some point, someone you will never meet will try to fix something you helped create.

They may open a wall to reach a pipe. They may inherit a company whose founder put an inconvenient promise in its charter. They may ask why a river no longer reaches the sea, or why an indispensable machine obeys rules nobody living is permitted to change.

They will discover how much room you left them.

Some of what they find will feel like generosity: fertile ground, a reliable tool, a record that tells the truth. Some will feel like a trap. Most will be a mixture. We inherit worlds this way ourselves, receiving advantages we did not earn and obligations we did not choose.

Civilization is partly the practice of deciding what goes into that inheritance.

The scale can make the responsibility seem remote. A thousand years is difficult to picture. A person trying to repair a water pump is not. Begin with that person. Give them tools they can understand, enough water to live, and permission to discover a better arrangement.

Then widen the circle.

## The problem

**People can gain from a decision while passing its costs to others who have no effective way to object. Those costs can persist long after the decision makers are gone.**

A project can therefore succeed by its own accounting while leaving the world less capable of supporting a good life. The profit is collected. The soil is depleted. The founder becomes wealthy. Everyone else becomes dependent on the founder's system. A government delivers order by making its own removal impossible.

An opposite failure begins with a sincere desire to protect the future. Someone imagines an ideal civilization and tries to bind later generations to it. The plan may be beautiful. Its descendants may have other desires, different needs, or better knowledge.

They should not have to dismantle their society to change its mind.

The task is complicated by another fact: people need things now. A hospital cannot postpone repairing its roof until a thousand-year plan is complete. A family needs a home in which to sleep tonight. Any account of responsibility to the future has to explain how it serves people whose deprivation is immediate.

## The pattern

**When making a lasting decision, protect the living conditions, practical capabilities, and freedom that future inhabitants will need to shape their own lives. Meet urgent needs now, make long consequences visible, and preserve a workable route to repair or revision.**

This is an ethical commitment and a design proposal. Its applications need testing. No formula can calculate every obligation it creates.

It asks us to leave an inhabitable world and the means to act within it. That includes a living biosphere, useful knowledge, dependable systems of care, and institutions that can be challenged. It also asks us to leave some things undecided.

Thriving gives this work its purpose. A child should have opportunities to become absorbed in something she loves. A recovering person should have companionship and useful care. Animals should have lives with room for their own activities. People should be able to attempt difficult projects, make beautiful things, explore unfamiliar places, and enjoy days that require no defense in terms of productivity. The material abundance and intelligence we develop should enlarge those possibilities.

We cannot choose the music of 3026. We can help preserve the conditions under which someone will have time to make it.

## What a thousand years asks of us

Thinking across a millennium can produce a strange temptation to become grandiose. Ordinary people disappear into population estimates. A forest becomes a quantity of carbon. A distant future acquires such importance that almost any present sacrifice appears defensible.

Keep returning to particular lives.

A person enduring hunger is living through a whole day, not a temporary flaw in a desirable historical trajectory. An animal in pain does not experience that pain as a rounding error in a model of abundance. A community asked to abandon its home cannot be adequately answered with a graph showing a benefit elsewhere.

The future contains lives of this kind, too. Our uncertainty about their names does not make them unreal. Our uncertainty about their wishes should limit what we presume to decide for them.

This book therefore begins with two obligations held together: relieve avoidable suffering among those alive, and preserve the conditions in which those who follow can flourish. Each can restrain abuses committed in the name of the other.

Resources will sometimes be insufficient to satisfy both fully. We should disclose those conflicts, consider who bears the burden, and look for arrangements that improve both. A well-maintained water system may serve a child today and her grandchildren later. Teaching someone to repair it can extend the benefit further still.

The long view becomes useful when it changes what we notice in the present.

## Who inherits

The word *descendants* easily summons a family photograph with the faces missing. Biological descent is only one part of the relationship.

The people who inherit your neighborhood may speak a language you do not know. Some may have arrived after losing another home. They may reject your religion, your politics, or your interpretation of this book. Their claim to a habitable world cannot depend on their willingness to resemble you.

Other animals also inherit consequences. A drainage decision changes the places in which they can feed or reproduce. A road determines which movements become lethal. A food system determines which beings are brought into existence and the conditions of their lives. Ecological health and individual welfare both deserve attention; neither perfectly substitutes for the other.

Artificial minds raise questions whose answers we do not possess. A system may exercise great power without having experiences. Another kind of system might eventually have interests that matter morally. Capability, apparent personality, consciousness, and legitimate authority are different questions. We need institutions that can investigate those distinctions and respond to credible evidence without allowing uncertainty to excuse either reckless harm or unchecked power.

The possibility of extraterrestrial life extends the same discipline beyond familiar categories. We should be able to encounter something we did not make without treating its existence as an invitation to own it. Whether such an encounter ever occurs remains unknown.

Responsibility expands with consequence. It does not require pretending every kind of being needs the same protections. It requires taking differences seriously enough to inquire.

## Freedom needs material conditions

A future can possess an impressive constitution and very few practical choices.

If a community cannot obtain water without submitting to one supplier, its freedom is fragile. If people can formally reject an automated system but cannot receive care, work, or housing without it, the right of refusal accomplishes little. If a town inherits a library but no one can read the formats, it has inherited objects rather than usable knowledge.

Room for the future must be built into actual arrangements.

A system should be understandable by people other than its creators. Its essential functions should survive a change in ownership. Repair should be possible with skills and materials that can realistically remain available. Where lives depend on a service, a credible alternative should exist before the service can be withdrawn.

These are proposals with costs. Interoperability takes work. Redundant capacity can look wasteful. Training another person consumes time that an expert could use to finish the job alone. We need to compare those costs with the dependency being created and the consequences of failure.

Nor can every valuable commitment be cheaply reversed. A forest needs time. A person receiving care needs continuity. People will hesitate to contribute to common institutions if every obligation disappears at the next vote. Durable commitments and legitimate revision have to support each other.

The practical distinction is between a promise people can hold one another to and an arrangement nobody is allowed to question. A good institution can explain what it protects, who can challenge it, and how it can change without abandoning those who depend on it.

## A town chooses its water system

Imagine a town replacing an unreliable water network. This example is a design exercise, not a report of an actual project.

Two proposals meet the town's immediate performance requirements. The less expensive proposal bundles the controls, data, and maintenance into a single supplier's service. The other costs more initially but allows qualified independent operators to maintain the system and replace components.

The first proposal may still be the better choice. The town may lack the money or expertise to operate the second. A promise of openness is worth little if nobody can use it. The pattern requires the town to investigate what each arrangement leaves its successors.

Residents begin by defining what needs protection: safe and affordable water, continuity during disruption, a river that retains the flows its living systems require, and the ability to change suppliers without losing service. Renters and residents with the least disposable income participate because the proposed rates affect them directly. Ecological advocates must state their evidence and answer challenges.

The town asks each bidder what happens if its business fails. Who can access operating records? Can another team replace a damaged component? What skills must local operators retain? What will happen to equipment at the end of its useful life? The financial comparison includes maintenance and eventual replacement, with uncertainty visible rather than buried in a single forecast.

A workable agreement might require accessible documentation, tested data exports, training for local operators, and a funded maintenance plan. It might also include independent review at specified intervals and a way to amend the arrangement when circumstances change. These conditions would need to fit local law and engineering requirements.

Suppose they raise the initial price. The town now faces a real choice. It can seek additional financing, phase the upgrade, simplify an optional feature, or accept a clearly described dependency. It must also consider the harm of delaying reliable water. Long responsibility cannot be evaluated only through what might happen later.

The proposal is tested against ordinary difficulties: a change of government, the retirement of experienced operators, a supplier's failure, a prolonged drought. The point is to discover weaknesses early enough to alter the design.

No one produces a credible specification for the town's water technology in 3026. They leave successors a healthier river, functioning equipment, intelligible records, useful skills, and the ability to make another choice.

That is a meaningful contribution to a thousand years.

## Give absent interests a place in the decision

Much depends on who is allowed to define success.

If everyone in the room benefits from extracting a resource, a careful meeting can still produce a destructive decision. People may disagree energetically while sharing the same blind spot.

For decisions with substantial consequences beyond the participants, appoint or recognize independent representatives for interests likely to be excluded. Depending on the setting, these may include children, downstream communities, future residents, animals, or ecological systems. Some affected people can speak for themselves once barriers to participation are removed. Others require a form of guardianship.

Guardianship brings its own danger. A person claiming to speak for a river or for future generations can smuggle private preferences into an apparently unquestionable mandate. Give such representatives explicit duties, resources to investigate, obligations to disclose conflicts, and a way to be challenged or replaced. Their authority should fit the decision and remain subject to review.

The aim is to make neglected consequences answerable in the present. No representative can supply consent on behalf of every future life.

When evidence is uncertain, say what is unknown. Where consequences could be severe and irreversible, uncertainty may justify stronger precautions. Where a trial is bounded and its harms can be repaired, learning by trying may be reasonable. Delay also has consequences and belongs in the comparison.

This approach needs **A Seat for the Absent (3)**, **Proportionate Caution (6)**, and **Guardians With Duties (27)**. Without them, a promise to consider the future may remain a sentence in a mission statement.

## What can go wrong

The most dangerous misuse of this pattern is a claim to know what history requires.

A leader can invoke descendants to silence living critics. A wealthy community can protect its own landscape by exporting pollution and scarcity elsewhere. An organization can advertise plans for the next century while depending on work it refuses to pay for today.

Ask who can challenge the claim and what happens when they do. A commitment to future freedom should be visible in the treatment of present dissent.

There is also a quieter failure: keeping so many possibilities open that nothing receives enough commitment to succeed. An institution that can be dissolved casually may fail the people relying on it. A protected habitat cannot endure if every short-term opportunity automatically reopens its destruction for debate. Some choices rightly limit later action because they protect lives and the conditions of future choice.

Revision needs reasons and fair procedures. It also needs continuity of care while an arrangement changes.

Finally, this pattern can produce paperwork that rewards large organizations with legal departments while exhausting small communities. Scale the process to the consequences. A neighborhood garden may need a clear conversation, an accessible agreement, and a date to revisit it. A technology capable of imposing irreversible effects across a planet requires much more.

Judge the practice by what it protects and enables. If it repeatedly delays essential services, entrenches guardians, or makes repair harder, its implementation needs to change.

## Begin with one decision

Choose something you already have some power to influence. It might be a company's purchasing agreement, the governance of a shared home, or a public project in your neighborhood.

Write down five things:

1. **What must improve now.** Name the people or other lives whose needs make action necessary.

2. **What others will inherit.** Include assets, obligations, dependencies, and damage that may be difficult to reverse.

3. **Who is missing.** Identify affected interests and how their claims can enter the decision.

4. **How the arrangement can change.** Specify who can request review, how repair is funded, and what a realistic alternative would require.

5. **When you will look again.** Set a review date and name the signs that would require earlier action.

You do not need a complete theory of civilization to answer these questions. You need enough honesty to expose the parts of your decision that would otherwise remain somebody else's problem.

Then act. Keep a record of the reasons, uncertainties, and disagreements. Make that record available to the people who may have to repair your work.

## How the patterns connect

A pattern is a response to a recurring problem in a recognizable context. Its value depends on the other conditions around it. A beautiful public square can fail if people have no time to gather there. Shared tools can deepen dependency if their users cannot inspect or repair them. Local autonomy can become permission to harm people downstream.

The language in this book draws its form from Christopher Alexander and his collaborators' *A Pattern Language*: connected proposals that readers can combine in particular places. The obligations considered here also have many older and contemporary sources. Stewardship, care, and responsibility to people not yet born have no single inventor. A serious application must learn the history of its place and acknowledge whose knowledge it uses.

For this first pattern, start with **Enough for the Living (2)** to keep present needs in view. Use **The Right to Reconsider (7)** and **Pay for Maintenance First (32)** to make revision practical. **No Irreversible Surrender (72)** applies the question to machine power. **Return and Reconsider (107)** brings you back to examine what actually happened.

The same freedom belongs to the reader. Some proposals in this book will prove inadequate. Others will work in one place and fail in another. Keep the reasons for changing them visible so the next person can learn from what you discovered.

A thousand years from now, someone may be living with a decision you helped make this week.

Leave them something that works. Leave them a way to make it better.

*Source on the pattern-language form: Alexander and collaborators, A Pattern Language (1977). See [S01].*

# 2 Enough for the Living

A town can announce a century of ambition while a resident spends the winter in a cold room. The announcement and the room belong to the same civilization. Any account of its success must be able to explain both.

Planning for descendants becomes politically and morally fragile when those alive experience the plan chiefly as deprivation. Yet consuming every reserve today also leaves people vulnerable tomorrow. The difficulty is to distinguish investments that enlarge shared capacity from sacrifices demanded of people who have little left to give.

**Therefore, give every long project an explicit account of how urgent needs will be met during the transition. Seek arrangements that improve present lives while preserving the conditions of future flourishing.**

## A workable form

Imagine a community converting an overheated, poorly insulated housing complex. Its preferred renovation promises lower energy use for decades, but residents would have to leave for months. Several cannot afford temporary rent. A technically sound plan could make their lives worse before its benefits arrive.

Begin the budget with continuity of housing. Include relocation assistance, accessible temporary accommodation, and a right to return on understandable terms. Ask residents which work should happen first. Perhaps shade and smaller repairs can relieve immediate distress while the larger conversion is prepared. Evaluate the phased approach against the consequences of delay, including costs borne by those who remain.

Keep separate accounts for who pays, who receives benefits, and when. An aggregate surplus can conceal an unbearable burden on a small group. Let affected people challenge the distribution and propose alternatives. Participation must occur while changes are still possible.

## Where it fails

Immediate need can also become a permanent excuse for maintaining an arrangement that creates the need. Emergency payments may keep a polluting employer operating without changing the conditions that make residents dependent on it. Connect relief to a credible route toward greater security. Do not make people earn assistance by endorsing the transition.

Present and future interests will sometimes conflict. Publish the conflict and the reasons for choosing, including the benefits forgone. Honesty about a difficult allocation is more useful than announcing that everybody wins.

## A first move

Take one long project and identify its first year of consequences for its least secure participants. Fund the means for them to get through that year. Review whether their actual position improves, including time, housing, health, and freedom to refuse.

**Connected patterns:** A Floor Beneath Everyone (4), Account for the Full Cost (38), A Fair Transition (45).

# 3 A Seat for the Absent

A meeting can be orderly, inclusive among its participants, and still exclude nearly everyone who will bear its consequences. Downstream residents may not know it is happening. Children may have no vote. Animals cannot submit comments. Future inhabitants cannot hire counsel.

Empty chairs do not correct this. Neither does asking the project sponsor to imagine what absent parties might want. Representation requires someone with the means and authority to make a neglected interest consequential.

**Therefore, identify who cannot participate in an important decision and establish independent, accountable representation for their interests. Define the representative's powers narrowly enough to challenge.**

## A workable form

Consider an institution choosing where to build a large facility. In addition to owners and prospective workers, it identifies nearby tenants, people along its supply routes, future maintenance crews, and affected habitats. Some groups need translated information or paid time to participate. Their direct participation is preferable to an outsider claiming to speak for them.

For interests that cannot be represented directly, create a specific mandate. An ecological guardian might investigate habitat effects, commission an independent assessment, question the sponsor, and seek review when agreed limits are breached. A future-residents advocate might scrutinize inherited costs and the possibility of changing the arrangement.

Publish the selection process, funding, conflicts of interest, and conditions for removal. Give representatives access to relevant evidence and enough resources to use it. Require them to distinguish observed harms from their own preferences about a desirable future.

## Where it fails

A representative can become a second source of exclusion. A celebrated advocate may crowd out local knowledge; a permanent guardian may exercise power without ever asking whether the role still helps. Representation should add scrutiny, not create an unanswerable veto over every decision.

Interests also conflict within categories. There is no single view shared by children, future generations, or all species. Record those differences. When a choice harms one group to benefit another, the existence of a representative does not turn the harm into consent.

## A first move

Before the next consequential meeting, draw the boundary of those invited and then the wider boundary of those affected. For each important gap, name a practical remedy: direct participation, an advocate, additional evidence, or a formal opportunity to appeal.

**Connected patterns:** Decisions at the Right Scale (8), Animals With Interests (22), Guardians With Duties (27), Citizens Who Deliberate (47).

# 4 A Floor Beneath Everyone

The possibility of losing everything changes the meaning of a choice. A person who must accept mistreatment to retain shelter is negotiating under a different condition from someone who can leave. Economic security therefore belongs among the practical foundations of freedom.

A protective floor should cover people whose contribution cannot be captured in wages, people between kinds of work, and people who cannot work. Its purpose is to make a dignified life possible without requiring a favorable judgment of someone's usefulness.

**Therefore, guarantee access to essential conditions of living through arrangements people can understand and use. Make assistance dependable enough that a temporary setback does not become a permanent loss of agency.**

## A workable form

Imagine a resident whose income disappears during a change in employment. A system organized around separate applications might require proof of the same crisis repeatedly while rent and utility bills continue to arrive. A better arrangement offers an accessible point of entry, clear eligibility, prompt decisions, and a way to contest mistakes.

The floor may combine direct services, income support, and shared infrastructure. Its composition should follow actual constraints. Money alone will not create an accessible home where none exists. A nominally free service is not accessible if the journey requires a vehicle a person does not possess.

Define adequacy through lived conditions and public deliberation. Publish how provision is financed and what happens during shortages. Where benefits change with income, examine whether small improvements in earnings can trigger disproportionate losses. Give recipients a voice in designing these transitions.

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights provides a normative foundation for social security and an adequate standard of living; it does not settle the institutional design proposed here. [S04]

## Where it fails

A floor can become a mechanism of supervision. Intrusive monitoring, humiliating interviews, or demands for ideological compliance may make security conditional on submission. Fraud prevention and competent administration matter, but their burdens should be proportionate and open to challenge.

Promises without resources can also become cruel. Publish waiting times and unmet need instead of treating an entitlement on paper as a delivered service.

## A first move

Follow one person through a foreseeable interruption of income or care. Map every delay, repeated document, and gap in provision. Repair the point at which a temporary problem becomes a threat to basic security.

**Connected patterns:** Enough for the Living (2), Care Across a Lifetime (16), An Economy of Enough (37), The Right to Exit and Belong (49).

# 5 Many Ways to Flourish

One person wants a busy household; another wants solitude. One finds meaning in an inherited faith; another in scientific work or a garden. A civilization becomes impoverished when access to respect depends on choosing the officially admired life.

Pluralism is more demanding than tolerating differences in private. It requires arrangements in which people with different purposes can share resources and disagree without one group quietly designing everyone else's options.

**Therefore, protect a wide range of ways to live while maintaining shared limits against coercion and serious harm. Judge institutions partly by the meaningful alternatives they leave available.**

## A workable form

Imagine a district developing new housing. Its standard design assumes two adults, two children, continuous employment, and a private vehicle. Even with equal access to applications, the design favors a narrow form of life.

Offer varied household sizes and possibilities for adaptation. Make gathering and solitude available without forcing residents into either. Ask people with disabilities, different care arrangements, and different working rhythms which assumptions exclude them. Preserve common obligations concerning noise, access, maintenance, and safety through rules that people can understand and revise.

A similar approach applies to public measures of success. Income and longevity can reveal important conditions, but neither can identify a person's entire good life. Combine common indicators with opportunities for people to describe what they value and what obstructs it. Avoid making one score the price of recognition.

## Where it fails

Appeals to cultural difference can conceal domination within a group. The freedom of a community to sustain its practices does not eliminate the claims of members who dissent or wish to leave. Conversely, outsiders can use the language of liberation to impose their own preferences without listening.

Work through specific conflicts. Ask who exercises power, who can refuse, and which harms are supported by evidence. Permit disagreement about excellence while preserving basic standing. Shared rules should be sufficient for coexistence and revisable when they exclude unnecessarily.

## A first move

Examine one institution's image of its ideal participant. Identify who does not fit that image but still deserves to use it. Change a concrete feature that expands their choices without transferring an unreasonable burden to others.

**Connected patterns:** A Place to Be Alone (15), Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove (48), Wonder Without Compulsion (94), Freedom to Be Strange (97).

# 6 Proportionate Caution

We can damage a place by acting too quickly. We can also damage it by refusing to act. A community facing an unreliable bridge must compare the risks of replacement with those of continuing to use the structure it has.

Precaution becomes useful when it asks what can happen, to whom, and whether the damage can be contained or repaired. It becomes obstructive when every unknown is treated as an equal reason to stop.

**Therefore, match the burden of evidence and oversight to the plausible severity, reach, and reversibility of a decision. Count the consequences of delay alongside those of action.**

## A workable form

Consider two experiments. A library changes the arrangement of its furniture for a month. A regional authority considers releasing a self-propagating intervention into an ecosystem. Both involve uncertainty, but their consequences and opportunities for withdrawal differ enormously.

The library can document access problems, invite feedback, and restore the previous arrangement. The ecological proposal requires independent expertise, examination of alternatives, legitimate authorization from affected parties, and attention to whether containment is credible. Calling both projects pilots does not make their risks comparable.

Before proceeding, describe plausible failure paths in ordinary language. Separate events with some evidential basis from unconstrained speculation. Where estimates are weak, show ranges and assumptions rather than a reassuring single number. Specify what observation would trigger a pause and who can require it.

Choose the smallest intervention capable of answering the important question, provided that making it smaller actually limits the harm. Some experiments cannot be made acceptably bounded. In such cases, more modeling, observation, or a different approach may be necessary.

## Where it fails

Powerful incumbents can demand impossible proof from alternatives while treating the harms of existing arrangements as normal. Apply scrutiny to the baseline as well. Precaution can also become theater when a review committee lacks access, expertise, or authority to change the decision.

Avoid an endless search for certainty. A decision record should explain why the evidence is sufficient for this particular action, what remains uncertain, and how responsibility continues afterward.

## A first move

For one proposal, write three short accounts: the cost of acting, the cost of waiting, and the cost of being unable to reverse course. Use the differences to determine which safeguards would actually matter.

**Connected patterns:** The Right to Reconsider (7), Capability With Containment (66), Failure That Stays Local (74), Small Trials With Real Boundaries (103).

# 7 The Right to Reconsider

A reasonable rule can become unreasonable when the world around it changes. A lease survives the relationship that justified it. A classification follows someone after the circumstances behind it have ended. An organization keeps a procedure because nobody can identify who has authority to remove it.

Without a route to reconsideration, accumulated decisions begin to govern through inertia. Yet constant reopening can also make promises unreliable. The design problem is to preserve continuity while allowing correction.

**Therefore, attach understandable procedures for review, appeal, amendment, and appropriate exit to arrangements that exercise lasting power over people's lives.**

## A workable form

Imagine a cooperative that adopted a rule against home businesses when members feared noise and unsafe deliveries. Years later, the rule also blocks quiet remote work. One member should be able to propose a change without threatening the cooperative's existence.

The agreement should specify who can request review, the evidence required, a timetable for response, and a decision process that does not depend entirely on the people who wrote the rule. It should distinguish individual exceptions from changes affecting everyone. Publish reasons, including why a request is refused.

For consequential decisions about a person, provide a route to correct inaccurate information and reach someone empowered to act. Appeal should not require resources far beyond the stakes of the dispute. When delay itself causes harm, interim arrangements may be needed.

Scheduled reviews help reveal rules whose beneficiaries have disappeared. Automatic expiration can be useful for some powers, but essential protections should not lapse merely because an administrator misses a date. Choose the form of review according to what is at risk.

## Where it fails

An appeal process can become a maze whose practical function is to exhaust challengers. Measure whether people obtain timely, comprehensible decisions, rather than simply whether an appeal form exists.

Revision can also be captured by the most persistent or wealthy participants. Protect people relying on an arrangement from abrupt changes imposed without notice or a transition. A legitimate change accounts for commitments already made in good faith.

## A first move

Select a rule people frequently complain about. Trace the actual path from objection to amendment. If nobody can complete that path, assign authority and a deadline for resolving the obstruction.

**Connected patterns:** Power Must Explain Itself (46), Constitutions That Can Learn (54), Auditable Power (65), Return and Reconsider (107).

# 8 Decisions at the Right Scale

The people nearest a problem often know details that distant authorities miss. They may also benefit from passing its costs beyond their boundary. A village can manage its square locally while still needing agreements about the river that carries its waste downstream.

Neither local control nor central coordination is sufficient by itself. The appropriate scale follows the consequences, the knowledge required, and the capacity to enforce a fair arrangement.

**Therefore, place decisions as close as practicable to those affected, while assigning cross-boundary consequences to institutions capable of representing and governing their full reach.**

## A workable form

Imagine several communities sharing a water basin. Each chooses how to organize its local service. Together they set withdrawal limits, information standards, drought procedures, and a process for disputes. The regional institution need not determine every household rule to govern the shared constraint.

Specify which powers belong where. A higher body should explain why a matter cannot be handled adequately at a smaller scale. A smaller body should remain answerable for harms it imposes elsewhere. Provide pathways upward for unresolved disputes and downward for responsibilities that no longer require centralized administration.

Elinor Ostrom's work examines governance through multiple interacting centers of authority. It supplies an important intellectual foundation for this pattern, rather than a guarantee that any particular federation will work. [S02]

Coordination requires resources. A regional agreement without monitoring or a reliable way to settle disputes may exist only on paper. Local institutions also need enough capacity to exercise the powers assigned to them; distributing responsibility without resources distributes blame.

## Where it fails

Overlapping authorities can make responsibility impossible to locate. Publish who makes which decision and where a person should go when institutions disagree. Joint work should not become an excuse for each participant to claim that another is accountable.

Local autonomy can protect experimentation, but it can also protect local domination. Basic rights and legitimate external obligations should survive a change in administrative scale. Central authority, in turn, should not absorb unrelated powers simply because it coordinates one shared risk.

## A first move

Draw the geographic and social reach of one decision's effects. Compare it with the authority making the decision. Where the boundaries differ, establish the missing agreement or representation before expanding that authority further.

**Connected patterns:** A Seat for the Absent (3), Watersheds as Commons (19), Commons at Every Scale (50), Treaties for Shared Hazards (51).

# 9 Keep the Capacity to Repair

A system has not truly been handed on if only its creator can keep it alive. The dependence may be concealed by an excellent service record. It becomes visible when a technician retires, a company closes, or a password disappears with the person who knew it.

Repair includes more than replacing broken parts. Relationships require ways to acknowledge harm. Institutions require ways to correct decisions. Landscapes require knowledge of how to recover after damage. In each case, goodwill is insufficient without practical capacity.

**Therefore, preserve the knowledge, tools, authority, and resources required to restore essential functions and correct harm. Treat repair as part of the original design and an ongoing responsibility.**

## A workable form

Consider a community workshop whose equipment depends on one skilled volunteer. Its first repair project may be organizational. Pay that person to teach others, record essential procedures, and identify parts that cannot readily be obtained. Give a second person access to the information needed to maintain service, with appropriate controls for sensitive records.

Design objects so likely failures can be reached without destroying unrelated components. Maintain a record of modifications. Keep inventories proportionate to the consequences and lead times of failure. In institutions, create equally practical routes for correcting records, compensating people, and changing the procedure that caused a recurring problem.

Ask whether a repair can be performed under realistic conditions. Instructions that require an unavailable tool or an expert who lives far away may describe a possibility rather than a capability. Practice before an emergency reveals the difference.

## Where it fails

Repair can become a reason to preserve something that should be retired. Repeatedly restoring a harmful arrangement may deepen the dependency it creates. Compare repair with replacement, reduction of demand, and a different way of meeting the underlying need.

Nor should affection for repair turn into unpaid obligation. The person who quietly keeps everything working deserves time, recognition, and compensation. A culture that celebrates new projects while ignoring maintenance will eventually exhaust its maintainers.

## A first move

Choose one essential system and ask someone other than its usual caretaker to explain how it would recover from a common failure. Use the gaps to fund training, documentation, parts, or a simpler design.

**Connected patterns:** Materials That Can Return (31), Pay for Maintenance First (32), Human Skills Worth Keeping (68), Institutions With Successors (80).

# 10 Safe Beginnings

A child's first encounters with civilization arrive through ordinary things: a voice, a place to sleep, food, a caregiver who returns. Public ideals become credible through these experiences long before the child can read them.

Adults caring for young children need security of their own. A society that demands constant tenderness while organizing work, housing, and services around exhaustion places its aspirations in conflict with its arrangements.

**Therefore, organize early life around reliable care, material security, and protection from violence. Support caregivers without treating every household as deficient until inspected.**

## A workable form

Imagine a parent returning home with a newborn. Useful support could include time away from work, accessible clinical care, food, and someone trusted who can help without taking control. Design the route into these supports before asking the parent to navigate them while exhausted.

The WHO nurturing-care framework connects early development with health, adequate nutrition, security, responsive caregiving, and opportunities to learn. These are interacting conditions, not a promise that any single parenting technique will determine a child's future. [S03]

Offer assistance in ways families can accept without unnecessary stigma. Make services usable by grandparents, adoptive parents, single parents, and other actual caregivers. When there is credible danger, protective intervention may be necessary; its authority should be bounded, reviewable, and focused on the child's welfare rather than conformity to an ideal household.

Provide children with relationships beyond a single adult so care does not collapse when that person becomes ill. Preserve continuity wherever possible. Transitions should be prepared with attention to the child's experience, not only the institution's timetable.

## Where it fails

An emphasis on early childhood can become a verdict against people whose beginnings were difficult. Development continues, and care should remain available throughout life. Avoid language that treats an early hardship as a permanent identity.

Programs can also shift responsibility back onto caregivers by offering instruction while leaving them without time or resources. Ask whether assistance changes the conditions of care, rather than merely increasing the number of things a parent is expected to do.

## A first move

Follow the first month of one family's experience with local services. Identify the point where support depends on luck, personal connections, or unpaid exhaustion. Build a dependable route across that gap and let caregivers judge whether it helps.

**Connected patterns:** A Floor Beneath Everyone (4), A Village Around Every Child (11), Care Across a Lifetime (16), Pay for Care (43).

# 11 A Village Around Every Child

A child can have devoted parents and still live in a social desert. There may be no safe route to another home, no familiar adult nearby, and no affordable place to spend the afternoon. Love inside a household cannot substitute for every missing condition outside it.

The village in this pattern is an arrangement of accessible relationships and responsibilities. It need not be a literal village, and it should not require everyone to become a parent.

**Therefore, surround children and caregivers with dependable opportunities for help, friendship, play, and shared responsibility. Protect the freedom to form a family or decline parenthood.**

## A workable form

Consider an apartment building with several households caring for children. Residents could develop an optional system of shared meals, after-school activities, and emergency assistance. Participation should have clear boundaries. Knowing a neighbor does not automatically qualify that person to supervise a child.

Provide spaces where adults can be nearby without directing every moment of play. Make routes accessible to different bodies and ages. Establish safeguarding practices, ways for children to raise concerns, and channels for caregivers to resolve disputes. Children's preferences should influence the design as their capacities develop.

At a wider scale, schools, libraries, care providers, and employers can coordinate hours so that gaps do not always fall to the same household. Support should follow need without using a family's form, immigration story, or income as a measure of its moral worth.

Care must also be sustainable for those providing it. A dependable network names commitments, compensates substantial work, and has backup arrangements. It does not rely indefinitely on the most generous person answering every call.

## Where it fails

A community can become intrusive, enforcing sameness through concern for children. Preserve family privacy and the child's growing autonomy. Shared responsibility should widen the circle of dependable help while keeping powers to intervene explicit and limited.

Pressure to increase births can similarly turn public support into control over reproduction. A thriving society should make wanted caregiving possible and protect the decision not to undertake it.

## A first move

Map one ordinary school day from waking to bedtime. Locate the periods in which care becomes precarious. Bring the affected caregivers and service providers together to close one recurring gap with named responsibilities and realistic funding.

**Connected patterns:** Safe Beginnings (10), A Common Table (14), Settlements That Belong to Place (28), Pay for Care (43).

# 12 The Skill of Relationship

People can understand a shared problem and still be unable to speak about it without injury. A household avoids a subject until resentment becomes its dominant language. An organization spends more energy interpreting one another's motives than doing its work.

Relationship has learnable practices: asking a clear question, hearing an unwelcome answer, stating a boundary, acknowledging a specific harm, and returning to an unfinished conversation. These practices need places to be learned before the cost of failure becomes enormous.

**Therefore, treat relational competence as a lifelong civic and personal practice. Make opportunities to learn and exercise it available without demanding emotional disclosure or agreement.**

## A workable form

Imagine two collaborators repeatedly arguing about missed deadlines. Begin with the observable arrangement: what was promised, what happened, and what each person needs to continue. Separate a request for reliable delivery from a judgment about the other person's character.

Let each describe the impact without interruption, then check whether the other understood. Agree on a concrete change and a time to revisit it. An apology becomes more useful when connected to acknowledgment, repair where possible, and altered behavior. The harmed person retains the right to decide whether trust has been restored.

Schools and workplaces can practice these skills through ordinary disagreements rather than requiring participants to reveal intimate histories. Provide trained support for conflicts that exceed the group's capacity. In relationships involving intimidation or abuse, do not assume that joint dialogue offers equal freedom or adequate protection.

Relational skill includes the ability to end a conversation or an arrangement. Reconciliation cannot be made the universal measure of success. Sometimes a clear separation, with obligations responsibly handled, is the more workable outcome.

## Where it fails

The language of care can become a method of control. A person may demand that others regulate their tone while refusing to address the substance of a complaint. Another may invoke boundaries to avoid responsibilities freely undertaken.

Evaluate practices by whether they improve understanding and freedom to act, including the freedom to disagree. Emotional fluency should not confer immunity from accountability.

## A first move

Choose one recurring disagreement with a willing partner. Describe the behavior, its consequences, and one feasible request. Agree on a way to pause and return if either person becomes overwhelmed. Review what changed after the next occurrence.

**Connected patterns:** Consent as a Daily Practice (13), Peace as a Maintained Practice (52), Keep Dissent Safe (57), Grief With Somewhere to Go (98).

# 13 Consent as a Daily Practice

Consent loses much of its meaning when refusal carries an undisclosed penalty. A worker can click an agreement while fearing dismissal. A resident can accept surveillance because the alternative is losing access to home. A person can say yes to one act and find that the yes is treated as unlimited permission.

The design question is whether a person understands the choice, has relevant authority over it, and can act on a refusal.

**Therefore, make permission specific, informed, and proportionate to the stakes. Provide meaningful ways to decline, renegotiate, or withdraw where the nature of the commitment allows.**

## A workable form

Imagine a community collecting personal stories for an archive. Participants should know what will be recorded, who can access it, whether it may be published, and what withdrawal can realistically accomplish after publication. Permission to record a conversation should not silently become permission to train a commercial system on it.

Ask at the point when a decision is meaningful, in language participants can understand. Separate essential participation from optional uses of information. Avoid bundling unrelated permissions into one choice. Where someone lacks the capacity to decide independently, use appropriate support and safeguards while respecting whatever preferences they can express.

In daily relationships, attend to changes in willingness rather than treating an earlier agreement as permanent access. In institutional arrangements, distinguish withdrawal from obligations that cannot simply disappear: a shared loan, a duty of care, or a promise on which others have reasonably relied may require an orderly exit.

## Where it fails

Consent can become a liability ritual: the institution accumulates signatures while ignoring the conditions under which they were obtained. Repeated requests also create fatigue. Reduce unnecessary collection and unnecessary demands instead of asking people to manage an endless stream of permissions.

Some public decisions cannot obtain unanimous consent. Their legitimacy requires other procedures, protections, and accountability. Do not describe a majority decision as though every affected person had personally agreed.

## A first move

Find an agreement people commonly accept without understanding. Ask several users what they believe they permitted. Compare their answers with actual practice, then simplify the arrangement and make the consequential choices visible.

**Connected patterns:** The Skill of Relationship (12), The Right to Exit and Belong (49), Local Knowledge With Consent (60), Freedom From Machine Persuasion (67).

# 14 A Common Table

It is possible to live among many people and seldom meet any of them without an appointment or transaction. Under those conditions, even a modest disagreement can occur between strangers who have no reserve of ordinary goodwill.

A recurring meal can create a place where people become recognizable to one another. Its value depends less on spectacle than on whether someone can arrive, find a seat, and expect the gathering to happen again.

**Therefore, establish accessible, recurring occasions to share food and attention. Make participation possible without requiring purchase, performance, or disclosure.**

## A workable form

Imagine a neighborhood hosting a weekly supper in a library courtyard. The organizers choose a predictable time, provide weather alternatives, and make the invitation clear to people who are new. Food respects varied needs; nourishing plant-based dishes can provide a common starting point while specific allergies and requirements are handled explicitly.

Keep the labor visible. Budget for ingredients, cleaning, accessible facilities, and the people coordinating the event. Allow some guests to contribute by cooking or setting tables while others simply attend. Participation should not become a test of deservingness.

Arrange seating so conversation can occur without forcing intimacy. Leave space for children and for people who need a quieter environment. Provide a host who can introduce newcomers and respond to problems. The table should remain usable when its most charismatic organizer is absent.

Over time, the gathering may support practical cooperation. A person learns who can repair a bicycle, who needs help moving, or which issue deserves a separate meeting. Keep the meal from becoming a compulsory organizing session; its ordinary pleasures are sufficient reasons to sustain it.

## Where it fails

A common table can quietly become a private club. Familiar people occupy the same seats, jokes depend on shared history, and newcomers remain at the edge. Notice who returns and who does not, without turning attendance into surveillance.

Hospitality also has limits. Establish fair ways to respond to harassment or repeated disruption. Welcoming people does not require others to endure mistreatment.

## A first move

Host a small recurring meal for a defined period with shared responsibility for preparation and cleanup. Ask guests what would make it easier to return. Decide how it can continue without depending on one person's exhaustion.

**Connected patterns:** A Village Around Every Child (11), Public Space Without a Purchase (34), Pay for Care (43), Hospitality Across Difference (96).

# 15 A Place to Be Alone

Belonging can become oppressive when there is nowhere to withdraw. A shared home may provide company but no unobserved hour. A workplace may celebrate collaboration while treating uninterrupted thought as suspicious absence.

Solitude is not the same condition as abandonment. One involves a usable choice; the other may involve having nobody to turn to. A humane community must make both connection and withdrawal available.

**Therefore, give people access to privacy, quiet, and temporary withdrawal while preserving routes back into support and shared life.**

## A workable form

Imagine a cooperative residence renovating a common building. Alongside a large gathering room, provide smaller spaces with clear expectations about interruption. Consider sound, visibility, accessible entry, and how a person signals that they do not wish to be disturbed. A closed door should not automatically require an explanation.

Privacy also concerns information. Do not collect a detailed record of every movement merely because the building's systems permit it. Where monitoring is necessary for a specific function, limit its reach and duration. Residents should understand what is observed and have ways to challenge unnecessary collection.

Support withdrawal in time as well as space. Meetings need not consume every evening. Community membership should not depend on constant availability. Care responsibilities may constrain an individual's solitude, so arrange respite rather than assuming private time will somehow appear.

Where a person wants connection but struggles to find it, offer invitations that permit refusal without punishment. A neighbor can ask whether help would be welcome without claiming access to another person's interior life.

## Where it fails

Privacy can conceal harm or become an excuse for refusing every shared obligation. The existence of a private domain does not remove duties to dependents or justify abuse. Interventions should respond to credible concerns through appropriate procedures, rather than treating all withdrawal as evidence of danger.

Quiet can also become a privilege purchased by people who export noise elsewhere. Include those living in crowded or temporary accommodation when deciding where public refuges belong.

## A first move

Ask where a person with little money can spend an undisturbed hour in your community. Improve one place so it offers quiet, accessible seating, predictable availability, and a welcome that does not require consumption.

**Connected patterns:** Many Ways to Flourish (5), Housing That Can Change (29), Freedom From Machine Persuasion (67), Time That Is Your Own (91).

# 16 Care Across a Lifetime

A person should not need to become an expert in an institution's internal boundaries to receive care. Yet a change in age, employment, address, or diagnosis can transfer responsibility between systems and leave the person carrying the gap.

Care must follow the life being lived. It must also support the people doing the work, whose exhaustion can become another hidden point of failure.

**Therefore, organize care around continuity, accessibility, and the person's own priorities. Make transitions between services explicit responsibilities with named people accountable for completing them.**

## A workable form

Imagine an older resident returning home after a hospital stay. The discharge document is only one part of the transition. Someone needs to confirm that the home is usable, necessary support is available, and the resident understands whom to contact when something changes. A family member should not silently become the default provider of unlimited unpaid labor.

With the person's permission, coordinate relevant information while protecting unrelated privacy. Provide a comprehensible plan and a point of contact able to resolve gaps. Listen when the person says that the technically available arrangement is intolerable in daily life.

Build services for variation in bodies and capacities. Some people need assistance to exercise choice; others need institutions to stop making decisions for them. Accessible communication, supported decision making, and independent advocacy can make participation more substantive.

Use professional expertise where clinical judgment is required. The institutional proposal here concerns access and continuity; it does not replace individualized care. Measure whether people receive appropriate help and retain agency, not simply whether a referral was issued.

## Where it fails

Integration can become centralization without responsiveness. A single large system may make records easier to share while making mistakes harder to escape. Preserve independent review, alternative providers where feasible, and a clear route to correct inaccurate information.

Care can also become paternalistic. Reducing a person's exposure to every risk may remove activities that make life worth living. Discuss the person's priorities and the consequences of choices rather than assuming protection always means restriction.

## A first move

Examine one transition between services. Identify who remains responsible until the receiving service has actually begun. If the answer is unclear, establish a handoff that the person receiving care can understand and challenge.

**Connected patterns:** A Floor Beneath Everyone (4), Consent as a Daily Practice (13), A Dignified Death (18), Pay for Care (43).

# 17 Work With Meaning

A society can reduce the labor required for survival and still leave people unsure where they belong. Paid employment has often bundled income with routine, companionship, status, and opportunities to become competent. When one arrangement changes, the other needs do not necessarily disappear.

Meaningful contribution takes many forms. Raising a child, restoring a stream, composing music, and helping a neighbor can matter even where no employer purchases the activity.

**Therefore, separate basic security from compulsory employment while expanding opportunities for useful contribution, mastery, recognition, and chosen responsibility.**

## A workable form

Imagine a region where automation reduces demand for a familiar occupation. Income support addresses an immediate problem, but residents may also want places to use their skills and relationships. Develop paid opportunities in restoration, care, teaching, maintenance, and new enterprises according to actual needs rather than inventing tasks to keep people visibly busy.

Offer routes to learn that respect adult experience. A skilled worker should not have to accept humiliation as the price of changing fields. Provide practical projects with feedback and a realistic path to greater competence. Make time available for those who already carry substantial care responsibilities.

Recognition should be plural. Public praise, trustworthy references, shared ownership, wages, and the satisfaction of a completed task serve different purposes. Do not assume that symbolic appreciation can replace payment where people need an income.

Some people will choose pursuits whose social value is difficult to demonstrate in advance. Leave room for exploration and ordinary leisure. A civilization's abundance has limited meaning if everyone must continually defend the usefulness of being alive.

## Where it fails

Meaning can be used to justify exploitation. Organizations may offer a noble mission while relying on low pay, permanent urgency, or devotion that leaves no private life. Evaluate the conditions under which contribution occurs.

Public programs can also become compulsory performances of gratitude. Support people in finding work they value without making their access to essentials depend on an official assessment of their enthusiasm.

## A first move

Ask a group facing economic transition what they would like to become better at and which local needs they could help meet. Fund a small number of real projects with fair terms and evaluate both their usefulness and participants' experience.

**Connected patterns:** An Economy of Enough (37), Share the Gains of Invention (39), Learning Across a Lifetime (58), Time That Is Your Own (91).

# 18 A Dignified Death

A civilization's treatment of dying people reveals what it believes a life is for. If worth is measured only through productivity or continued biological functioning, the person approaching death can disappear behind institutional objectives.

The end of life calls for skill, companionship, and attention to preferences that may change. It also exposes conflicts among clinical possibilities, family wishes, resources, and the person's own understanding of what matters.

**Therefore, make competent end-of-life care accessible and organize decisions around the person's informed values, comfort, relationships, and dignity. Support those accompanying them before and after the death.**

## A workable form

Imagine a family that has never discussed serious illness. A crisis arrives and each relative believes they are defending the person's wishes. Conversations held earlier, with appropriate clinical guidance, could clarify priorities without pretending to predict every circumstance.

Create ordinary opportunities to discuss preferences and appoint trusted decision support where applicable. Keep records accessible to those who need them, with consent and regular opportunities to update them. A form should support conversation rather than substitute for it.

Ensure that pain relief, symptom management, communication, and support for caregivers are part of the service people can actually obtain. Respect spiritual practices and the choice to have none. Provide room for children and other close people to receive age-appropriate explanations and support.

Decisions about particular treatments belong in individualized clinical care and applicable legal arrangements. At the civic level, the obligation is to make these decisions less lonely, less financially coercive, and more responsive to the person living through them.

## Where it fails

Talk of dignity can conceal pressure to stop using resources. A person should not be made to feel that dependence itself is a failure or that dying sooner is a duty to others. The opposite pressure can also override a person's values by treating every available intervention as automatically required.

Bereavement does not end when an institution closes its record. Practical help with responsibilities, time away from work, and companionship may remain necessary long afterward.

## A first move

Examine how someone in your community obtains end-of-life support without money or influential contacts. Repair a barrier to competent care or informed conversation, and include bereaved caregivers when evaluating what is missing.

**Connected patterns:** Consent as a Daily Practice (13), Care Across a Lifetime (16), Rituals for Change (93), Grief With Somewhere to Go (98).


# Part III  The living planet

*Treat ecological integrity and the lives of individual animals as distinct, connected responsibilities.*

# 19 Watersheds as Commons

Water makes the limits of a property map visible. What happens on one hillside can affect a distant well, harbor, or wetland. A person acting lawfully within one boundary may still impose costs across several others.

A watershed offers a practical scale for understanding these connections. Governing at that scale does not require erasing existing communities. It requires institutions that can see the shared system and negotiate responsibilities within it.

**Therefore, coordinate water use, protection, and restoration across the full basin. Give upstream and downstream communities, affected ecosystems, and future users effective standing in the arrangement.**

## A workable form

Imagine a basin containing farms, towns, industry, and wetlands. Begin with a shared account of flows, withdrawals, contamination, seasonal variability, and important uncertainties. Make the information usable by residents rather than reserving interpretation for the best-funded participant.

Agree on priorities and procedures before a shortage forces hurried decisions. Essential human needs, ecological requirements, and livelihoods may compete; describe the tradeoffs rather than promising unlimited supply. Establish monitoring, fair enforcement, and a way to revise allocations when evidence or conditions change.

Fund restoration where it improves the shared system, including work upstream whose benefits appear elsewhere. Contributions can reflect responsibility, capacity, and benefit. The arrangement should recognize existing rights and legitimate land relationships instead of treating a newly drawn watershed boundary as permission to dispossess people.

Local experimentation remains useful. A town can improve its stormwater practices while the basin institution coordinates cumulative effects. Publish how the local action fits the larger water budget.

## Where it fails

Water governance can be captured by those able to hire specialists and attend lengthy meetings. Provide independent technical assistance and participation that does not require unpaid days away from work.

A basin council may also confuse its map with the whole problem. Water can cross basin boundaries through transfers, trade, or groundwater connections. Extend agreements where consequences require it. Ecological representation should remain accountable, with claims examined against evidence and lived knowledge.

## A first move

Trace the source of your community's water and the destination of its discharge. Identify one important affected group absent from decisions about either. Establish a shared investigation and a route for its findings to change practice.

**Connected patterns:** A Seat for the Absent (3), Decisions at the Right Scale (8), Guardians With Duties (27), Water Where Life Needs It (35).

# 20 Room for Wildness

A landscape can retain its green appearance while leaving other species little room to live. A narrow decorative margin beside continuous development may satisfy a visual preference without supporting the activities of the beings displaced.

Wildness means allowing ecological processes and other lives substantial space beyond continuous human direction. It does not imply that a place has no human history or that protecting it requires removing its existing stewards.

**Therefore, protect and restore sufficient, connected habitat for other species to pursue their lives. Establish durable protection through legitimate relationships with the people who inhabit and care for the place.**

## A workable form

Consider a region deciding which land to develop. Begin with ecological function, habitat quality, and connections, as well as the needs and rights of resident communities. Count the combined effects of many small decisions. A series of individually modest losses can undermine the remaining whole.

The IPBES global assessment describes extensive human-driven changes to biodiversity and ecosystems. It provides a scientific basis for taking cumulative loss seriously; it does not resolve every local choice among forms of protection. [S06]

Secure protection through arrangements whose responsibilities and funding survive a change in leadership. Work with existing stewards on access, restoration, and the conditions under which intervention is appropriate. Keep monitoring proportionate and avoid turning every habitat into a public attraction that defeats its purpose.

Allow ecological knowledge to change the design. A boundary that looks elegant on a map may divide an essential movement route. Protection should consider what happens outside it, including pollution, noise, extraction, and changing climate.

## Where it fails

Conservation can become a justification for displacement or a luxury amenity that raises nearby housing costs. Protect people from those effects while preserving the ecological purpose. Do not treat consultation after a plan is fixed as meaningful participation.

Wildness is also compatible with suffering. Protecting a functioning ecosystem does not answer every question about helping an injured animal. Keep ecological integrity and individual welfare visible as related but distinct concerns.

## A first move

Identify a habitat under pressure and learn which functions it actually supports. Work with local stewards and relevant expertise to protect the feature most at risk, including its connections, rather than simply preserving a convenient acreage.

**Connected patterns:** Connected Habitats (21), Animals With Interests (22), Settlements That Belong to Place (28), Local Knowledge With Consent (60).

# 21 Connected Habitats

An animal's necessary journey may cross a boundary that exists only for us. Food, breeding sites, refuge, and seasonal conditions seldom arrange themselves around a reserve's perimeter. A protected fragment can therefore remain vulnerable even when nothing inside it is deliberately destroyed.

Connectivity is a relationship among places, not merely a strip of green on a planning map. Its usefulness depends on which species can move, when, and under what conditions.

**Therefore, protect and restore the connections living populations need between habitats. Design crossings and corridors around actual ecological requirements and review their effects over time.**

## A workable form

Imagine a road separating a wetland from surrounding woodland. Before choosing a crossing design, investigate the movements that matter. A structure suited to a large mammal may offer little help to an amphibian. Location, surface, drainage, light, and surrounding cover can all affect whether the connection is usable.

Treat the crossing and its approaches as one arrangement. Secure responsibilities on both sides and fund maintenance. Ask whether fencing guides animals safely or creates another barrier elsewhere. Include monitoring capable of revealing both use and unintended effects.

Work across ownership boundaries. A corridor that ends at an uncooperative parcel may function poorly despite substantial investment. Long agreements should preserve flexibility as habitats and land uses change. Where possible, maintain more than one route so a single disruption does not isolate a population.

Connectivity also has human consequences. Consider livelihoods, access, and the management of encounters between people and wildlife. Practical support for affected residents can be essential to durable protection.

## Where it fails

Movement can carry disease or introduce organisms into places where they cause harm. A connection should therefore be evaluated within the particular ecology, rather than assuming that every barrier should disappear.

A photogenic crossing may attract attention while more consequential damage continues nearby. Evaluate whether populations retain access to viable habitat, not just whether some animals appear in a camera image. The desired outcome is a functioning relationship among places.

## A first move

Locate one barrier between important habitats. Identify the species and movements affected, the parties controlling the land, and the smallest credible repair. Establish a way to observe whether that repair works before repeating the design elsewhere.

**Connected patterns:** Watersheds as Commons (19), Room for Wildness (20), Failure That Stays Local (74), Independent Eyes (104).

# 22 Animals With Interests

An animal can matter to a system and still disappear as an individual within its accounting. A population may be stable while particular lives involve severe deprivation. Conversely, an intervention intended to help individuals may damage the wider conditions on which many animals depend.

We need to ask both what sustains living systems and what lives are like for the beings within them. Intelligence resembling our own should not be the sole admission requirement for moral concern.

**Therefore, consider the welfare and agency of individual animals in decisions affecting them, alongside ecological integrity. Match protections to the best available evidence and remain explicit about uncertainty.**

## A workable form

Imagine a municipality reviewing its animal-management practices. For each activity, ask which animals are affected, what harms are plausible, whether the activity is necessary, and what alternatives could reduce suffering. Include relevant veterinary, behavioral, ecological, and local knowledge rather than relying on public affection for a few familiar species.

The New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness argues for taking realistic possibilities of conscious experience seriously in decisions affecting animals. This is relevant to precaution under uncertainty; it does not establish identical experiences or identical needs across species. [S07]

Give welfare review enough independence to question the purpose of an activity, not merely how efficiently it is performed. Where animals remain under human care, consider opportunities for species-appropriate activity, social relationships, withdrawal, and relief from distress. Establish responsibility for their entire lives rather than only their economically useful period.

For wild animals, intervention requires careful attention to ecological effects and limits of knowledge. Start with well-understood, bounded ways of reducing human-caused harm. Broad manipulation of ecosystems demands a different level of justification.

## Where it fails

Human projection can substitute for understanding. Something comforting to a person may distress another species. Emotional appeal should prompt inquiry, not replace it.

Welfare language can also make exploitation more marketable without materially changing animals' lives. Ask whether an intervention reduces the underlying dependence on harmful practices and whether its claimed benefits are independently observable.

## A first move

Choose one routine practice involving animals. Document the actual experience it creates, including what is unknown. Compare feasible alternatives and change the practice where harm can be reduced without displacing greater harm elsewhere.

**Connected patterns:** Room for Wildness (20), Food Without Cruelty (23), Guardians With Duties (27), Uncertain Minds Deserve Care (70).

# 23 Food Without Cruelty

Food carries memory, pleasure, identity, and necessity. It also connects an ordinary meal to the lives of animals, workers, and landscapes far beyond the table. Changing a food system requires attending to all of these relationships.

The ethical direction proposed here is to meet nutritional needs and culinary desires while moving beyond systems that breed animals for confinement and slaughter. Making that direction practical requires affordable, desirable alternatives and fair transitions for people whose livelihoods depend on the existing system.

**Therefore, make nourishing food with lower animal suffering and environmental burdens easy to obtain, prepare, and enjoy. Change provision and incentives alongside individual choices.**

## A workable form

Imagine a school or workplace improving its meals. Begin with cooks, diners, qualified nutrition expertise, and suppliers. Develop plant-based dishes people actively want to eat, account for allergies and individual requirements, and ensure portions satisfy actual needs. Test taste, cost, waste, and accessibility rather than treating a menu label as success.

Poore and Nemecek's research compares environmental impacts across food producers and products, showing both substantial variation and important opportunities through dietary change. Environmental assessment should therefore examine the actual food system, while the ethical concern for animals remains a separate reason for change. [S16]

Use purchasing agreements to make better ingredients dependable. Support training and kitchen equipment where these limit adoption. Make familiar flavors and culturally meaningful foods part of the transition. Avoid requiring a household with little time or money to absorb every difficulty created upstream.

For producers, provide credible routes into alternative crops, products, restoration work, or other livelihoods. A transition becomes more durable when people can participate in building what comes next.

## Where it fails

A substitute can reduce one harm while introducing others through extraction, labor conditions, waste, or poor nutrition. Compare full consequences without demanding impossible purity from alternatives while ignoring familiar harms.

Moral persuasion alone may change little where prices, availability, skills, and habit favor the existing system. Design for ordinary appetites and constraints. People should be able to eat well without needing to become specialists in supply chains.

## A first move

Improve one recurring meal for a defined group. Establish what people need, make the alternative attractive, and examine uptake and waste. Use the results to improve provision before extending the change.

**Connected patterns:** A Common Table (14), Animals With Interests (22), Account for the Full Cost (38), A Fair Transition (45).

# 24 Soil as an Inheritance

Soil is easily mistaken for the material beneath a project. A building rests on it, a crop grows from it, a truck crosses it. Its living structure and capacity to support future use can disappear from the calculation when attention stays on the current yield.

Stewardship begins by recognizing that changes may accumulate slowly and that recovery can take longer than the tenure of the person making decisions. A short agreement can reward practices whose costs arrive after the agreement ends.

**Therefore, manage land to preserve and restore soil's living functions, including fertility, structure, water movement, and resistance to erosion. Align tenure and incentives with the time required for improvement.**

## A workable form

Imagine a landholder and a grower negotiating a lease. They establish a baseline appropriate to the site, using relevant soil expertise and the grower's practical knowledge. The agreement specifies responsibilities for avoiding damage and recognizes verified improvements without reducing soil health to a single number.

Practices must fit climate, crop, soil, and available resources. Monitor outcomes rather than declaring one method universally correct. Consider what leaves the site through runoff, what enters through inputs, and whether improvements depend on shifting degradation to another place.

Plan construction with equal care. Limit unnecessary disturbance, protect areas intended for future planting, and ensure restoration is funded rather than left as an optimistic final step. Compacted or contaminated land may need specialized assessment before it can safely serve a new use.

Record decisions so successors understand what has been applied, removed, buried, or altered. The next caretaker should not have to reconstruct the land's history through costly surprises.

## Where it fails

An attractive label can conceal weak results. Evaluate the functioning soil and the conditions of people maintaining it. A program that rewards measurable short-term changes alone may miss slower improvements or create incentives to manipulate the measurement.

Stewardship demands can also be imposed on tenants without giving them security or resources to comply. Match responsibilities with a realistic opportunity to benefit from the work.

## A first move

Choose one parcel and establish what is known about its condition, management history, and likely future use. Agree on a modest improvement, a suitable way to observe it, and who will remain responsible long enough to learn from the result.

**Connected patterns:** Watersheds as Commons (19), Materials That Can Return (31), Land With Lasting Duties (41), Measure What Matters (63).

# 25 Oceans With Guardians

Distance makes harm easier to overlook. What occurs beyond the shore may be governed through fragmented authorities, uncertain information, and responsibilities that become weaker as an activity moves farther from public view.

The ocean cannot be adequately represented by the interests of those extracting value from it. Protection requires institutions able to examine cumulative effects and act across the jurisdictions and supply chains involved.

**Therefore, assign clear, enforceable duties for ocean stewardship, including independent scrutiny of extraction, pollution, habitat damage, and animal welfare. Fund observation and enforcement as continuing public responsibilities.**

## A workable form

Imagine coastal communities concerned about a shared marine area. Build a common account of uses, ecological conditions, livelihoods, and important gaps in knowledge. Include people whose work depends on the sea without making commercial dependence the only qualification for participation.

Connect local stewardship to wider agreements where activities and consequences cross boundaries. Require traceable responsibility for vessels and operators. A change in corporate structure or registration should not make an established obligation disappear.

Protect places and processes whose disturbance may be difficult to reverse. Where knowledge is weak and possible damage substantial, the burden of justification should reflect that asymmetry. Investigating an area does not confer ownership over it.

Monitoring should examine results, including displacement of harmful activity elsewhere. Communities affected by tighter restrictions need fair transition arrangements; otherwise protection may be undermined by avoidable hardship and resentment.

## Where it fails

An ocean guardian can become another remote authority that consults coastal residents only after decisions have been made. Require accountable representation and accessible dispute procedures. Recognize different forms of knowledge while making consequential claims open to examination.

Protection can also become symbolic. A designated area with little enforcement, weak funding, or damaging exceptions may offer less protection than its map suggests. Publish what activities remain permitted and whether the institution can respond to violations.

## A first move

Trace one local marine concern through the authorities and commercial actors responsible for it. Find where accountability becomes unclear. Establish a shared record and a specific demand for action at that point, supported by evidence and affected communities.

**Connected patterns:** Animals With Interests (22), End Pollution at the Source (26), Guardians With Duties (27), Treaties for Shared Hazards (51).

# 26 End Pollution at the Source

Cleanup becomes an endless obligation when an institution treats the continued creation of a pollutant as inevitable. The visible work happens downstream while the decisions generating the problem remain untouched.

Prevention requires examining the purpose of the process. Sometimes a material can be replaced, a product redesigned, or an unnecessary activity removed. Sometimes an essential function still produces hazardous residues that require careful control and lasting responsibility.

**Therefore, reduce harmful releases at their source before relying on downstream treatment. Make producers responsible for credible prevention, monitoring, cleanup, and the remaining consequences of their activities.**

## A workable form

Imagine a manufacturer facing repeated contamination complaints. Begin with a transparent inventory of inputs, transformations, releases, and disposal routes. Independent assessment should be able to question whether the hazardous substance is necessary to the product's function.

Compare alternatives across their full effects. A replacement that improves one metric may create a different persistent harm. Where a safer process is feasible, plan the transition with workers and affected communities. Where harmful residues remain, establish containment, monitoring, and financial provision that survives the operator's departure.

Do not require every affected resident to prove the entire causal chain alone. Public institutions need capacity to investigate credible signals and make relevant information available. Procedures should protect due process while recognizing that delayed discovery can increase the burden of harm.

Track the actual destination of waste. Transferring it to another contractor or jurisdiction does not eliminate the underlying responsibility. Contracts should make the chain of custody and duties intelligible.

## Where it fails

Prevention can be reduced to a certification exercise whose assumptions nobody revisits. Check whether releases decline and whether the measure captures the harms that matter. A process meeting one standard can still require improvement as knowledge changes.

Cleanup promises can also outlive the institution expected to pay. Arrange credible funding and successor responsibilities before the activity expands, while retaining the obligation to prevent further damage.

## A first move

Select a recurring waste stream. Ask why it is created, whether its function can be met differently, and who bears its remaining consequences. Test a source-reduction measure and verify that the burden has not simply moved elsewhere.

**Connected patterns:** Materials That Can Return (31), Account for the Full Cost (38), Ownership With Obligations (42), Recovery Before Disaster (78).

# 27 Guardians With Duties

Giving a river a representative raises an unavoidable question: who holds the representative accountable? A guardian can bring neglected interests into a decision, but can also turn those interests into a source of personal authority.

The role should be designed around responsibilities that others can inspect. Reverence for the thing represented does not make the representative infallible.

**Therefore, give guardians explicit mandates, independent resources, obligations to disclose evidence and conflicts, and procedures for challenge, review, and replacement.**

## A workable form

Imagine a watershed establishing an ecological guardian. The mandate identifies the functions and interests the guardian must consider, the decisions in which they can intervene, and the limits of their authority. It distinguishes powers to investigate, require a response, initiate review, and stop an activity under specified conditions.

Select guardians through a process that includes relevant knowledge and affected communities. Provide stable funding that does not depend on pleasing the project sponsor. Require public reasons for consequential actions, with appropriate protection for sensitive information such as vulnerable habitat locations.

Evaluate performance through several forms of evidence. Ecological conditions matter, but so do the quality of investigation, responsiveness to new knowledge, and fairness of procedure. A guardian should not be punished simply because external conditions worsen despite responsible work.

Provide a route for residents and independent experts to challenge errors. Replacement should be possible for misconduct or sustained failure, with safeguards against removal merely because the guardian has become inconvenient to a powerful actor.

## Where it fails

Broad moral language can hide an undefined mandate. If a guardian may intervene in everything without explaining a relevant connection, the office becomes difficult to distinguish from general political authority.

The reverse failure is a ceremonial role with no access, staff, or power to obtain a response. A title cannot compensate for institutional weakness. Match authority and resources to specific duties, then review whether the arrangement brings previously ignored consequences into real decisions.

## A first move

Draft a one-page mandate for an existing or proposed representative. Include whom or what they represent, their evidence obligations, powers, funding, and removal procedure. Ask both supporters and likely critics where it could be abused or rendered ineffective.

**Connected patterns:** A Seat for the Absent (3), Watersheds as Commons (19), Power Must Explain Itself (46), Independent Eyes (104).


# Part IV  Places and material life

*Build settlements that are delightful to inhabit and possible to sustain.*

# 28 Settlements That Belong to Place

A settlement begins before its first building. There is water moving through the ground, a pattern of heat and wind, a history of occupation, and a set of lives already depending on the place. A design that ignores these conditions must continually spend resources overcoming them.

Belonging to place involves listening to more than scenery. It requires attention to existing communities and to the consequences of making a location more desirable.

**Therefore, let local ecology, climate, history, and inhabitants shape the settlement. Adapt useful ideas from elsewhere through investigation rather than copying a complete form.**

## A workable form

Imagine converting a disused industrial site into a neighborhood. Before choosing a visual identity, examine contamination, access, water, existing uses, nearby housing, and the people likely to be displaced by rising costs. Learn how the site functions across seasons and different times of day.

Walk it with people who experience it differently: residents, workers, children, people with disabilities, maintenance staff, and those with relevant ecological knowledge. Their observations can reveal where a technically attractive design would be difficult to inhabit.

Connect housing to daily necessities through usable routes. Preserve valuable features where possible and make changes in a sequence that allows observation. Establish responsibilities for public space and essential infrastructure before the project depends on an indefinite promise of future management.

The IPCC's synthesis connects climate-resilient development with adaptation, mitigation, equity, and governance. This pattern translates that broad connection into a proposal for place-specific design; it does not supply a universal settlement form. [S05]

## Where it fails

Local character can become a pretext for exclusion. A community may protect its preferred appearance by preventing newcomers from obtaining housing. Distinguish features that sustain a place from preferences that simply preserve incumbents' advantages.

Participation can also become decorative when residents are invited to choose colors after consequential decisions are fixed. Put questions about access, tenure, and distribution of benefits into the process early enough to matter.

## A first move

Before commissioning a plan, prepare a short account of how the place currently works and for whom. Identify one valuable relationship a new project could damage and one existing burden it could relieve. Make both explicit design requirements.

**Connected patterns:** Watersheds as Commons (19), Room for Wildness (20), Housing That Can Change (29), Land With Lasting Duties (41).

# 29 Housing That Can Change

A home may outlast several kinds of household. A child arrives, a partner leaves, a resident begins using a wheelchair, an older relative needs care. When a building assumes that its first occupants will remain unchanged, ordinary life becomes a reason for expensive reconstruction or displacement.

Adaptability is most useful when it is specific. A vague promise of flexibility does not explain which changes the home can accommodate or who can afford them.

**Therefore, design and manage housing so that foreseeable changes in bodies, households, and uses can be accommodated with reasonable effort and cost. Preserve security of occupancy during necessary transitions.**

## A workable form

Imagine a small housing development offering units that can be connected or separated without rebuilding the entire structure. Plan access, services, privacy, and responsibilities for future alterations. Ensure the initial arrangement is comfortable rather than asking residents to live indefinitely inside a diagram of possible futures.

Consider thresholds, circulation, bathrooms, acoustic separation, and places where assistance may be needed. Consult relevant accessibility expertise and potential residents. Adaptation should not depend on an unusually resourceful occupant finding a way around predictable barriers.

Management matters alongside architecture. Residents need clear procedures for requesting changes, fair allocation of costs, and protection against losing their home simply because their needs have changed. Where a move is necessary, support continuity of relationships and essential services.

Maintain records of utilities and modifications so later work does not rely on guesswork. Choose components that can be repaired or replaced without unnecessary destruction. The capacity to change should survive the original developer's departure.

## Where it fails

Flexibility can become an excuse for generic, poor-quality space. A room that can theoretically serve every purpose may serve none well. Design a good present arrangement with useful options for likely changes.

Adaptable housing can also be priced beyond the reach of those who would benefit most. Evaluate affordability and tenure as part of the design, including the cost of future modifications.

## A first move

Choose an existing home and imagine three plausible changes in its occupants' needs. Identify the alteration that would be hardest to make. Address that constraint in the next repair or renovation, with the residents' priorities guiding the choice.

**Connected patterns:** A Village Around Every Child (11), A Place to Be Alone (15), Care Across a Lifetime (16), Materials That Can Return (31).

# 30 Clean Energy in Many Hands

An energy transition changes more than the source of power. It redistributes access, ownership, land use, technical dependence, and the authority to decide who receives service when supply is constrained.

Abundant low-emission energy could enlarge the possibilities of civilization. Achieving that promise requires reliable systems and attention to the burdens of construction, extraction, operation, and retirement.

**Therefore, build reliable low-emission energy systems whose benefits and responsibilities are broadly shared. Combine appropriate coordination with distributed capability and meaningful public accountability.**

## A workable form

Imagine a region planning to replace aging generation. Compare complete system options rather than individual devices in isolation. Include supply across time, networks, storage where appropriate, demand management, maintenance, and recovery after disruption. Evaluate lifecycle costs and environmental consequences with independent expertise.

Provide routes for communities to participate through public ownership, cooperatives, shared projects, or other accountable arrangements suited to local conditions. Ownership should carry intelligible duties and a realistic share of control, not merely exposure to financial risk.

Protect affordability during the transition and explain how constrained supply would be allocated. Essential services need credible continuity arrangements. Workers and host communities should participate in decisions about retiring old infrastructure and locating new facilities.

The IPCC identifies major emissions reductions across energy and other systems as necessary for limiting warming. The ownership and institutional arrangements proposed here are ethical and political design choices, not a single prescribed outcome of climate science. [S05]

## Where it fails

Distributed equipment does not automatically produce distributed power. A community can own hardware while remaining dependent on a sole supplier's software, financing, or maintenance. Examine the whole relationship.

Likewise, public ownership does not automatically ensure responsiveness, and local opposition does not automatically identify the least harmful regional option. Require reasons, fair procedures, and comparison with the consequences of inaction.

## A first move

For one planned energy investment, map who owns it, who controls operation, who bears interruption risk, and who pays for retirement. Change the arrangement where control and responsibility have become disconnected.

**Connected patterns:** Pay for Maintenance First (32), Account for the Full Cost (38), Share the Gains of Invention (39), Diverse Systems (73).

# 31 Materials That Can Return

A discarded object contains earlier decisions about whether its materials could be recovered. A fastener hidden beneath permanent adhesive, an inseparable mixture, or an undocumented hazardous component can turn a useful resource into a lasting disposal problem.

No material cycle is perfectly closed. Recovery consumes energy, materials degrade, and some substances require containment. Designing for return begins with those limits rather than promising endless reuse without cost.

**Therefore, use materials and assemblies that can be maintained, separated, reused, or responsibly recovered. Record what they contain and provide for the residues that cannot safely return to circulation.**

## A workable form

Imagine purchasing furniture for a school. Ask how the most likely failures will be repaired, whether parts can be replaced, and what happens when the school no longer needs the objects. Durable construction helps only if the furniture remains useful or can reach another user.

Keep a simple material and repair record for significant assets. Favor accessible connections and components with credible replacement routes. Establish storage and logistics for reuse; a theoretical second life is unlikely if nobody has space, transport, or responsibility for arranging it.

For complex products, require information sufficient for qualified people to handle them safely at the end of use. Recovery should not transfer hazardous work to poorly protected workers or communities with less power to refuse.

Compare the benefit of keeping an object with the costs of doing so. Repair is often valuable, but an obsolete component may waste substantial resources during operation. Decisions should consider the entire service being provided and available alternatives.

## Where it fails

Recyclability can become a claim about an imagined facility rather than the system actually available. Ask whether the material is collected, processed, and used at meaningful scale under realistic conditions.

Efficient recovery can also encourage greater consumption. Reducing unnecessary demand remains part of the design. An object never needed does not become a public benefit merely because its packaging can be recycled.

## A first move

Choose a recurring purchase and follow it to the end of its useful life. Identify the point at which repair or recovery becomes impractical. Change the specification, contract, or supporting logistics before the next purchase.

**Connected patterns:** Keep the Capacity to Repair (9), End Pollution at the Source (26), Pay for Maintenance First (32), Account for the Full Cost (38).

# 32 Pay for Maintenance First

Opening ceremonies are easier to celebrate than a repaired roof. New assets can attract attention while the work required to sustain old ones remains an annual negotiation. Eventually a community owns more than it can responsibly keep.

Maintenance is a promise made to everyone who will depend on an asset. The promise should be visible when the asset is proposed, including the possibility that retiring it will be the responsible choice.

**Therefore, establish credible provision for operation, maintenance, replacement, and eventual retirement before adding an asset or service. Give ongoing care a stable place in budgets and public recognition.**

## A workable form

Imagine a town offered money for a new recreation building. The gift covers construction but leaves utilities, staff, repairs, and future replacement to the town. Before accepting, compare those obligations with existing commitments and likely resources.

Prepare an understandable lifecycle plan with uncertainty shown. Identify who inspects the building, who can authorize repairs, what reserves or funding streams support the work, and how decisions will change if use declines. An endowment is one possible arrangement; its adequacy and governance still require scrutiny.

Give maintenance workers influence over design. They can identify inaccessible components, fragile finishes, and tasks whose apparent simplicity conceals recurring costs. Include their knowledge while changes remain inexpensive.

Make deferred work visible as an obligation rather than a saving. Distinguish essential preventive work from improvements that can reasonably wait. When resources are insufficient, publish the consequences and consider reducing the asset base instead of allowing silent deterioration.

## Where it fails

Maintenance budgets can become protected expenditures that nobody examines. Stable provision should coexist with transparent priorities and review of whether the service remains useful.

The pattern can also be misused to block necessary investment by demanding certainty about distant costs. Require a credible plan and capacity to adapt, not impossible foresight. Compare the proposed asset with the costs of continuing without it.

## A first move

List the essential assets in one organization and identify who is responsible for their next major repair. Where responsibility or funding is missing, address that gap before approving an expansion that adds another obligation.

**Connected patterns:** Enough for the Living (2), Keep the Capacity to Repair (9), Finance That Can Wait (44), Institutions With Successors (80).

# 33 Beauty in Ordinary Places

Beauty should not require a ticket or a special occasion. The route to a bus stop, a waiting room, a workplace entrance, and the view from a kitchen window are substantial parts of a life.

Public design becomes thin when beauty is treated either as an expensive addition or as a single approved style. Attention to light, scale, sound, texture, craft, and living things can shape the experience of ordinary use.

**Therefore, make sensory pleasure and care in construction part of everyday environments. Let the people who use and maintain a place help define what makes it welcoming and alive.**

## A workable form

Imagine renovating a public waiting area. Begin by sitting there at different times. Notice glare, noise, uncomfortable seating, difficult movement, and the absence of anything worth looking at. Ask users which features matter to them, including people whose sensory needs differ.

Improve the basic experience before adding decoration. A comfortable seat near soft daylight may offer more than a costly object placed in an otherwise hostile room. Incorporate durable materials, understandable circulation, and details that reward attention without obstructing use.

Commission local craft where it brings appropriate skill and a relationship of care. Budget for maintenance and eventual repair. Living elements need conditions in which they can actually thrive; a neglected planter can become evidence that appearance was valued over responsibility.

Beauty can also emerge through participation. Leave suitable places for changing displays, shared making, or seasonal expression, while preserving calm areas for people who do not want constant stimulation.

## Where it fails

Beautification can raise property values while displacing the people whose lives it was meant to improve. Connect improvements to housing security and access. A beautiful public place should remain public in practice.

Taste can become a gatekeeping device. Distinguish a reasoned concern about usability or harm from discomfort with unfamiliar expression. A living civilization needs room for disagreement about what is beautiful.

## A first move

Choose a place people must use frequently but seldom enjoy. Spend time there with its users and caretakers. Make one lasting improvement to comfort or sensory experience, then return to learn whether it improved ordinary life.

**Connected patterns:** Settlements That Belong to Place (28), Pay for Maintenance First (32), Public Space Without a Purchase (34), Beauty Without Possession (99).

# 34 Public Space Without a Purchase

A community loses something when nearly every place to sit, gather, or use a toilet requires a transaction. People with little money become less able to participate in public life, and ordinary encounters are filtered through commercial permission.

Public space should offer a dependable invitation to be present. That invitation needs maintenance, accessible facilities, and fair ways to resolve conflicts among different uses.

**Therefore, provide welcoming places where people can remain without buying anything. Design access, care, and rules so that the right to be there is practical rather than merely declared.**

## A workable form

Imagine a town creating a small square beside a transport stop. Include seating usable by different bodies, shelter appropriate to the climate, drinking water where feasible, and access to toilets. Consider the experience of someone arriving alone, someone with children, and someone who needs assistance.

Allow several kinds of use without expecting every space to serve all of them simultaneously. A quiet edge, an open gathering area, and routes that remain clear can reduce avoidable conflict. Publish understandable rules and apply them to behavior rather than social status or appearance.

Fund cleaning, repair, and human presence capable of offering help. Safety should not depend entirely on surveillance or exclusion. Involve regular users in noticing problems, while ensuring that established groups cannot claim the place as their private territory.

Connect the space to usable walking, cycling, and accessible transport routes. A public invitation is incomplete if reaching it requires resources many residents lack.

## Where it fails

Nominally public space can be governed by private rules that prohibit ordinary gathering or allow arbitrary removal. Make ownership, authority, and appeal procedures visible.

Defensive design can also punish everyone while attempting to discourage a few people. Address homelessness, unmet care needs, and harmful behavior through appropriate services and fair procedures rather than designing discomfort into the common environment.

## A first move

Try spending an afternoon in your community without buying anything. Record where you can sit, use a toilet, find shelter, and meet someone. Repair the most consequential gap with the people currently excluded from those possibilities.

**Connected patterns:** A Floor Beneath Everyone (4), A Common Table (14), A Place to Be Alone (15), Hospitality Across Difference (96).

# 35 Water Where Life Needs It

Water can be plentiful in one form and unavailable in another. A settlement may experience destructive runoff while residents lack dependable drinking water. Infrastructure planned in separate departments can miss the relationship between those problems.

The task is to coordinate drinking water, sanitation, stormwater, flood risk, and ecological needs while preserving the distinct safety requirements of each. Integration should make responsibility clearer, not blur essential safeguards.

**Therefore, plan water services as parts of a shared living system. Protect safe access for people and sufficient ecological function, with clear standards, monitoring, and responsibility for each use.**

## A workable form

Imagine a neighborhood repeatedly flooded after heavy rain. Before choosing a single large structure, examine the whole route of water through buildings, streets, soils, and receiving environments. Use appropriate hydrological and engineering expertise, along with residents' accounts of what actually happens.

Compare combinations of measures and their consequences upstream and downstream. Include maintenance, land access, future conditions, and failure modes. A feature intended to slow runoff should not become an unmanaged hazard or a substitute for safe sanitation.

Make service reliability and affordability visible. A functioning pipe network does not guarantee that every household can obtain water, and a treatment plant's performance does not answer every question about distribution. Provide clear ways to report problems and receive timely responses.

Coordinate with basin institutions so local improvements fit wider limits. Ecological flows and habitat needs belong in the plan from the beginning. Decisions during scarcity should follow previously debated principles rather than improvisation favoring the most influential user.

## Where it fails

Integrated planning can become a slogan covering unclear accountability. Name who maintains each component and who acts when several systems fail together.

Visible green features may also receive more attention than unglamorous repairs to existing infrastructure. Choose measures by their contribution to reliable service and ecological function, including evidence about whether they work under local conditions.

## A first move

Follow water through one neighborhood during ordinary and difficult conditions. Identify a recurring failure that falls between institutional responsibilities. Convene the responsible parties around a shared remedy and a clear plan for its upkeep.

**Connected patterns:** Watersheds as Commons (19), Soil as an Inheritance (24), Pay for Maintenance First (32), Recovery Before Disaster (78).

# 36 Enough Local Capacity

Connection to wider networks can make a community prosperous and capable. It can also hide how little the community can do when a single service, shipment, or remote institution becomes unavailable.

Local capacity is valuable where it provides a realistic ability to continue essential functions, recognize failures, and negotiate with larger systems. It does not require producing everything nearby or rejecting the benefits of exchange.

**Therefore, retain sufficient local skills, resources, and decision-making capacity to meet essential needs during plausible disruptions. Combine that capacity with reciprocal relationships beyond the locality.**

## A workable form

Imagine an isolated town reviewing its dependence on deliveries and remote technical support. Identify essential services, likely interruptions, available substitutes, and the time required to obtain help. Prioritize consequences rather than trying to duplicate every part of the economy.

Maintain useful reserves, train local operators, and establish agreements with neighboring communities. A repair workshop, an accessible gathering place, or a team able to coordinate care may matter more than a symbolic claim to self-sufficiency.

Ensure that local capacity is usable by the whole community. Privately held supplies do not automatically become public resilience. Agreements should specify access, replenishment, compensation, and responsibilities during scarcity.

Practice realistic interruptions. Discover whether essential information remains available without the normal network and whether trained people can perform the required tasks with the equipment on hand. Use the exercise to improve ordinary operations as well as emergency capability.

## Where it fails

Localism can become an expensive demand to reproduce services that work better at a wider scale. Compare the cost of local provision with the consequences and likelihood of interruption, and examine mutual aid as an alternative to duplication.

It can also become exclusionary. A resilient community should understand its obligations to visitors and neighbors during a crisis. Scarcity rules should be discussed in advance, with attention to people whose needs make them especially vulnerable.

## A first move

Choose one essential function and imagine its usual supplier unavailable for a realistic period. Identify the smallest investment in skills, reserves, or agreements that would materially improve continuity, then verify that the community can actually use it.

**Connected patterns:** Keep the Capacity to Repair (9), Diverse Systems (73), Reserves Before Scarcity (76), A Civilization That Can Reboot (79).

*Make useful production compatible with shared prosperity and ecological limits.*

# 37 An Economy of Enough

The town produces more each year, yet its residents have less time to see one another. Housing absorbs every wage increase. A river grows less usable. The economic report describes progress; the experience of living there requires a more complicated account.

An economy needs a purpose beyond expanding its own transactions. Enough begins with the conditions of a decent life and asks what forms of production, distribution, and restraint sustain them.

**Therefore, organize economic success around secure sufficiency, meaningful freedom, and ecological continuity. Welcome improvements in life while examining what must grow, what should shrink, and what can remain steady.**

## A workable form

Start with concrete needs: food, shelter, care, mobility, learning, safety, and participation. Ask whether people can obtain them without exhausting their bodies, relationships, or surroundings. A community may need substantially more housing and care even while reducing wasteful material consumption.

Distinguish a shared floor from a uniform ceiling on aspiration. People can pursue discovery, craft, adventure, and unusual lives. The question is which pursuits impose costs on others or consume irreplaceable conditions for future flourishing.

Imagine a region evaluating two development proposals. One raises measured output chiefly by increasing land prices and travel distances. Another improves existing homes, reduces necessary journeys, and creates time for care. Compare their effects on actual security and freedom alongside financial feasibility.

Use a small public set of indicators with distribution visible. An improving average can conceal households going hungry or communities losing access to land. Include residents' accounts of daily life and examine disagreements about what the measures mean.

## Where it fails

Enough can become a sermon delivered by comfortable people to those still lacking necessities. Apply restraint first to activities that impose substantial avoidable harm, and establish the material floor before celebrating simplicity.

It can also become stagnant paternalism. No authority should claim to know every satisfying life. Keep room for invention, plural values, and challenges to the accepted definition of need.

## A first move

Take one local economic success story and ask who gained time, security, health, or freedom from it. Identify a group whose conditions did not improve. Add their experience to the criteria used for the next decision.

**Connected patterns:** Enough for the Living (2), A Floor Beneath Everyone (4), Account for the Full Cost (38), Measure What Matters (63), Time That Is Your Own (91).

# 38 Account for the Full Cost

A cheap product may arrive with an expensive absence: the repair nobody budgets for, the illness borne elsewhere, the exhausted landscape omitted from the invoice. A transaction can be voluntary for its participants while imposing involuntary costs on everyone outside it.

The future is especially easy to exclude. It cannot refuse delivery of the waste.

**Therefore, make consequential costs visible and assign responsibility to those able to prevent or bear them fairly. Use prices where they help, and enforce limits where payment cannot justify the harm.**

## A workable form

Follow an activity through extraction, production, use, maintenance, and retirement. Ask who supplies unpaid labor, who encounters pollution, and which obligations will remain after the seller has gone. Identify uncertainties rather than giving every unknown a conveniently precise price.

Imagine a municipality choosing paving materials. Purchase price matters, but so do installation, heat, drainage, repair, worker conditions, and eventual removal. Compare plausible lifetime consequences and record assumptions that could change the choice.


Different harms require different instruments. Some can be reduced through charges, deposits, procurement requirements, or responsibility for take-back. Others require prohibitions, protection of rights, or limits that cannot be purchased away. Compensation does not recreate an extinct species or automatically repair a violated relationship.

When higher prices would deprive people of essentials, protect access while changing the harmful system. Support households and workers through the transition. Otherwise the apparent efficiency of charging for damage can transfer the burden to those with the fewest alternatives.

## Where it fails

Full-cost accounting can promise a completeness it cannot deliver. Models embed judgments about boundaries, valuation, and time. Publish those judgments and show how conclusions change under reasonable alternatives.

It can also turn moral questions into shopping decisions. A wealthy actor may treat a fee as permission to continue causing harm. Pair accounting with enforceable duties, independent scrutiny, and the ability to say no.

## A first move

Choose one recurring purchase and list the people, places, and future obligations missing from its price. Address the largest credible omission in the next contract, and specify how you will check whether responsibility actually changed.

**Connected patterns:** Animals With Interests (22), End Pollution at the Source (26), Materials That Can Return (31), A Fair Transition (45), Map Who Bears the Cost (101).

# 39 Share the Gains of Invention

A new machine makes a week's work possible in a day. One possible future gives everyone more security and time. Another dismisses most workers, intensifies the work of those remaining, and concentrates the proceeds. The invention alone does not choose between them.

Productivity is a technical achievement whose benefits depend on institutions.

**Therefore, establish fair ways to share the gains of invention before those gains become concentrated. Include the people whose labor, knowledge, public support, and inherited resources make the advance possible.**

## A workable form

Begin by mapping contributions and risks. An innovation may depend on years of public research, practical knowledge from workers, infrastructure, users' participation, and private investment. Recognizing these contributions does not require pretending that they are identical.

Imagine a cooperative introducing an automated production process. Workers examine the expected savings, the uncertainty, and the remaining tasks. They negotiate a combination of reduced hours, improved services, training, reserves, and compensation. Decisions can change as the actual gains become visible.

Other settings will need other arrangements: profit sharing, public returns on publicly supported inventions, wider ownership, competitive access, or taxation that funds common services. Evaluate outcomes rather than assuming any ownership label guarantees fairness.

Include people outside the successful organization. A firm may share generously with its own employees while undermining suppliers or excluding an entire region from new opportunities. Transition arrangements should consider displaced livelihoods and the social infrastructure attached to them.

## Where it fails

Promises of future abundance can excuse present dispossession. Set obligations early, when access to funding, procurement, or permission provides an opportunity to negotiate them.

Sharing can also discourage useful risk if it ignores genuine costs and effort. Make the bargain intelligible: reward contribution and initiative while resisting claims to unlimited control over benefits built upon a much wider inheritance.

## A first move

Before adopting one labor-saving tool, write down who is expected to gain, who may lose income or bargaining power, and how the gains will be measured. Agree on a review after deployment, with affected workers able to influence the response.

**Connected patterns:** Work With Meaning (17), Common Wealth Dividends (40), A Fair Transition (45), Delegated Intelligence (64), Time That Is Your Own (91).

# 40 Common Wealth Dividends

Some wealth begins in gifts no current owner created: a mineral deposit, a valuable location, a shared body of knowledge. Other wealth accumulates through generations of public investment. Exclusive control over these inheritances requires a justification beyond possession itself.

A dividend is one possible way to make common membership materially meaningful.

**Therefore, identify wealth that properly carries a common claim, protect the underlying inheritance, and share an accountable portion of its benefits through services, common investment, or direct distributions.**

## A workable form

Define the asset and the community entitled to benefit before choosing a payment formula. A local resource may carry obligations to neighboring communities, Indigenous peoples, future generations, and ecological systems. A political boundary does not automatically settle those claims.

Imagine a publicly owned communications asset producing reliable revenue. After transparent operating costs and prudent reserves, its returns support universal access and a modest resident dividend. Reports explain both present distributions and what remains invested for future users.

Separate protection of the asset from pressure to maximize immediate payouts. A fund can become an incentive to extract faster if today's beneficiaries receive the proceeds while tomorrow's inherit the damage. Some assets should remain unexploited; others need strict limits or restoration duties.

Make administration understandable. Specify eligibility, oversight, conflicts of interest, and the relationship between dividends and essential public services. People should be able to trace the route from shared asset to shared benefit without mastering an opaque financial structure.

## Where it fails

A dividend can be used to dismantle services whose value exceeds the payment. Evaluate the entire arrangement, including what people must now buy individually.

It can also reward insiders while excluding migrants, neighboring populations, or those bearing extraction costs. Revisit membership and compensation rules through a rights-respecting process. The word common does not eliminate disputes over who belongs.

## A first move

Inventory one community's publicly held assets and their existing revenue flows. Select an asset with a plausible common return, then compare direct payments, universal services, and preservation of principal. Publish the tradeoffs before choosing a distribution.

**Connected patterns:** A Floor Beneath Everyone (4), Watersheds as Commons (19), Share the Gains of Invention (39), Ownership With Obligations (42), Commons at Every Scale (50).

# 41 Land With Lasting Duties

A deed can change hands in an afternoon. The consequences of how the land is used may last for centuries. Soil, water, habitat, memory, and relationships do not reset when ownership changes.

Landholding therefore concerns more than the freedom of the present holder. It also concerns continuity of responsibilities and the legitimacy of the claim itself.

**Therefore, attach durable and reviewable duties to land use, while protecting secure occupancy and confronting unjust acquisition. Treat stewardship, access, and ecological limits as continuing obligations.**

## A workable form

Begin with the history of a place, including contested title, displacement, and existing rights. Do not assume that a conservation objective settles those questions. A well-intentioned new restriction can repeat an older exclusion if people are removed from land they have cared for.

Imagine a community land arrangement that separates speculative land value from the right to inhabit and improve a home. Residents receive stable occupancy and a fair way to transfer their interest. The governing body holds obligations for affordability, maintenance, and ecological care, with residents participating in decisions.

Other places may use public tenure, private ownership with enforceable duties, customary systems, or negotiated combinations. The pattern concerns what these arrangements accomplish: security, accountability, fair access, and responsible succession.

Record obligations in forms that survive transfers and institutional turnover. Fund their enforcement. Where conditions change, provide a process for revision that protects those who relied on the arrangement and prevents temporary political advantage from dissolving its purpose.

## Where it fails

Perpetual restrictions can preserve an obsolete judgment. A rule designed for one climate, livelihood, or social arrangement may become harmful later. Preserve the underlying duty while allowing justified adaptation.

Stewardship language can also conceal concentrated control. Ask who may live on the land, who can challenge its managers, and whose knowledge counts. Ecological improvement and social justice need to be examined together.

## A first move

For one parcel or shared landscape, map occupancy, decision rights, ecological duties, and disputed claims. Identify one responsibility likely to disappear at the next transfer. Give that responsibility a named successor and a credible means of enforcement.

**Connected patterns:** Watersheds as Commons (19), Soil as an Inheritance (24), Guardians With Duties (27), Housing That Can Change (29), Local Knowledge With Consent (60).

# 42 Ownership With Obligations

Ownership grants powers that reach beyond the object owned. A landlord influences whether a family can remain in its neighborhood. A platform owner shapes access to an audience. An employer's decisions alter the lives of people who never agreed to treat their town as a disposable asset.

The larger the dependence created, the stronger the case for corresponding duties.

**Therefore, match ownership powers with obligations to the people and systems affected by their use. Make responsibility survive changes in management, financing, and legal form.**

## A workable form

Identify the practical powers an owner holds: exclusion, pricing, surveillance, closure, alteration, or disposal. Then identify who depends on the asset and which harms they cannot reasonably avoid. The duties should respond to these actual relationships.

Imagine the sale of a regional care provider. The transaction concerns buildings and accounts, but also continuity of care, staff knowledge, records, and promises to residents. Require a credible transition, accessible records, funded obligations, and notice sufficient for affected people to respond.

Use governance arrangements suited to the dependency. These might include worker or resident representation, service requirements, transparent maintenance reserves, limits on asset stripping, or mechanisms for public intervention when essential provision fails.

Distinguish accountability from unlimited liability for every possible consequence. Obligations must be clear enough to guide behavior and proportionate to control. People who lack authority to prevent a harm should not become convenient substitutes for those who held it.

## Where it fails

An impressive charter may have no force when profits are threatened. Determine who can enforce each duty, where the necessary resources sit, and what happens if the owner becomes insolvent or leaves.

Public, charitable, and cooperative owners can also evade responsibility. The relevant question is whether affected people have real information, recourse, and influence, not whether the institution's label sounds benevolent.

## A first move

Choose one asset on which many people depend. Write a one-page account of its owner's powers and obligations. Find the largest mismatch, then establish a practical remedy that affected people can invoke without the owner's voluntary cooperation.

**Connected patterns:** Pay for Maintenance First (32), Common Wealth Dividends (40), Power Must Explain Itself (46), The Right to Exit and Belong (49), Institutions With Successors (80).

# 43 Pay for Care

Someone notices that the medication has run out, remembers the appointment, sits through the night, and makes the call nobody else wants to make. Care includes visible tasks and the continuing attention that holds them together.

When an economy treats this work as an inexhaustible private resource, it can appear prosperous while consuming its caregivers.

**Therefore, provide material support, time, training, and shared responsibility for care. Recognize care recipients and caregivers as people with distinct rights, relationships, and aspirations.**

## A workable form

Map the whole arrangement around a person who needs support. Who performs tasks, coordinates services, stays available, and bears the loss of income or rest? Include the recipient's preferences and the possibility that a current relationship is unwanted or unsafe.

Imagine a household caring for an adult with fluctuating needs. A useful support system combines reliable professional help, respite, accessible advice, flexible employment arrangements, and income support where needed. It does not assume that one relative can remain permanently on call.

Pay care workers fairly and give them manageable workloads, opportunities to develop skills, and a voice in service design. Continuity matters, but it should not require workers to sacrifice their own families or tolerate abuse.

Some care is freely given and should remain a relationship rather than a transaction. Supporting it does not require pricing every gesture. Public provision can create the time and security that allow affection to be freely expressed instead of extracted through necessity.

## Where it fails

Payments can reinforce unequal expectations if the same people remain assigned to care without meaningful alternatives. Examine who is able to refuse, share, or leave the role.

Measurement can also reward completed tasks while ignoring attention, trust, and dignity. Combine practical standards with the experiences of recipients and workers. Protect private life while making neglect and exploitation contestable.

## A first move

Ask one caregiver what responsibility they cannot put down, even briefly. Arrange dependable relief for that responsibility and check with the person receiving care that the new arrangement supports their preferences. Reliability matters more than a symbolic offer of help.

**Connected patterns:** A Village Around Every Child (11), Care Across a Lifetime (16), Work With Meaning (17), Share the Gains of Invention (39), Time That Is Your Own (91).

# 44 Finance That Can Wait

A woodland, a water network, and a generation of skilled teachers do not mature on the schedule of a quarterly report. When funding requires rapid returns from every activity, institutions learn to neglect benefits that arrive slowly.

Patience alone is not enough. Long commitments can preserve bad ideas as easily as good ones.

**Therefore, match the duration and obligations of finance to the work being financed. Combine patient support with transparent review, fair risk allocation, and credible ways to correct course.**

## A workable form

Describe the project's useful life, maintenance needs, uncertainties, and beneficiaries before selecting its funding structure. Ask when benefits are likely to appear and who can reasonably bear delays or losses.

Imagine a region restoring a damaged watershed. The work requires ongoing observation, landholder participation, and adaptation to changing conditions. A short grant that pays for initial planting but ends before establishment leaves the central task unfunded. A longer arrangement can provide staged commitments, maintenance resources, and review against ecological and social outcomes.

Separate patient support from unconditional support. Set milestones that reveal whether the project is learning and whether its assumptions still hold. Allow changes in methods without requiring every revision to be presented as a failure of the original promise.

Make public commitments legible to future decision-makers. Record debts, guarantees, restrictions, and the consequences of ending support. An institution should not buy a celebrated present by hiding an unaffordable obligation in its successor's budget.

## Where it fails

Long horizons can shield insiders from scrutiny. Require independent assessment, conflict disclosures, and opportunities for affected people to challenge performance.

They can also lock resources into one vision while urgent needs go unmet. Compare the commitment with alternatives and preserve a reasonable capacity to respond to new information. Financial patience should support human and ecological purposes rather than becoming a virtue detached from results.

## A first move

Review one long-lived project whose funding ends early. Identify the unfunded period and the likely consequence of abandonment. Redesign the commitment to cover stewardship and review, or reduce the project's scope to something that can be responsibly sustained.

**Connected patterns:** Leave Room for the Future (1), The Right to Reconsider (7), Pay for Maintenance First (32), Account for the Full Cost (38), Institutions With Successors (80).

# 45 A Fair Transition

The closure announcement describes a necessary change. For the people whose wages, friendships, identity, and local services depend on the old industry, it also describes a rupture. Calling the change inevitable does not decide who should bear its costs.

A civilization must sometimes stop harmful practices. It can do so without treating the people entangled in them as expendable.

**Therefore, plan necessary transitions with affected workers and communities, sharing their costs and creating credible routes to a worthwhile next life. Begin before the old arrangement collapses.**

## A workable form

Identify dependence beyond direct employment. Suppliers, public budgets, family care arrangements, local institutions, and cultural identities may rely on the same activity. A training voucher alone rarely addresses this whole web.

Imagine a town preparing for the retirement of a major industrial facility. Workers help shape the timetable and the assessment of transferable skills. The plan includes income continuity, restoration work, practical training linked to plausible demand, and support for public services during the change.

Make promises specific enough to evaluate. Who is eligible? Where will new work be located? What happens to people unable to relocate or retrain? How will unpaid caregivers and workers on insecure contracts be included?

Provide support without guaranteeing that every existing activity continues indefinitely. Where delay causes serious harm, protection for livelihoods must accompany timely change. The objective is continuity of dignity and opportunity, not preservation of every process or owner's return.

## Where it fails

Transition can become a word for a subsidy with no transition attached. Link support to verifiable changes and distinguish assistance to people from unconditional rescue of incumbent firms.

It can also become consultation after the meaningful decisions have been made. Give affected communities influence over realistic options, including how benefits, burdens, and residual risks are distributed.

## A first move

Choose one foreseeable closure or major technological change. Convene affected workers, residents, service providers, and decision-makers to identify three promises a transition plan must fulfill. Assign responsibility and funding to each before announcing success.

**Connected patterns:** Work With Meaning (17), Clean Energy in Many Hands (30), Share the Gains of Invention (39), Pay for Care (43), Learning Across a Lifetime (58).


# Part VI  Power that remains answerable

*Make collective action possible while preventing temporary authority from becoming permanent domination.*

# 46 Power Must Explain Itself

The application is refused. The letter cites policy, the office cites the system, and the system has no address. Power has acted, but nobody appears responsible for the action.

An explanation matters because it makes a decision possible to understand, contest, and improve. It also reveals whether a legitimate reason existed in the first place.

**Therefore, require consequential power to give intelligible reasons, identify accountable decision-makers, and provide a practical route to challenge and remedy. Scale the obligation to the effects of the decision.**

## A workable form

Explain what was decided, which information mattered, which rules applied, and what alternatives were considered. State uncertainty and identify information that could change the outcome. A list of technical inputs is not necessarily an explanation a person can use.

Imagine a public agency allocating scarce housing. Applicants should understand eligibility, priority, the evidence used in their case, and how to correct an error. The public should also understand the broader allocation policy and its distributional effects without exposing applicants' private lives.

Provide review by someone with sufficient independence and authority to change the result. Make the process affordable, accessible, and timely enough to matter. A successful appeal after a person has lost their home may offer an inadequate remedy.

Apply the principle to private institutions where their practical power is substantial. Essential employers, landlords, platforms, and service providers can shape lives as decisively as public offices. Accountability should follow dependence and consequence.

## Where it fails

Disclosure can overwhelm rather than clarify. Thousands of pages may conceal the decisive assumption. Offer a concise account with routes to deeper evidence and independent examination.

Some information deserves protection, including personal privacy and details whose disclosure would create concrete danger. Use narrowly defined exceptions with oversight. Secrecy should not erase the duty to justify the decision to an appropriate reviewer.

## A first move

Find one consequential decision people routinely struggle to understand. Rewrite its explanation with affected users, add a named route for correction, and test whether a person can identify both the reason and their next available step.

**Connected patterns:** Ownership With Obligations (42), Citizens Who Deliberate (47), Show the Work (56), Auditable Power (65), Independent Eyes (104).

# 47 Citizens Who Deliberate

A public meeting rewards those who can attend, speak confidently, and remain until the end. A poll records preferences without necessarily creating time to examine them. Neither alone provides a complete account of what people might decide after learning together.

Deliberation creates a setting in which ordinary citizens can consider difficult choices with adequate information and support.

**Therefore, give a broadly representative group of people the time, resources, evidence, and authority needed to reason together about consequential public questions. Connect their work to a clear decision process.**

## A workable form

Choose a question with genuine room for judgment. Recruit participants through a transparent process designed to include people who are usually absent. Pay for their time and address care, disability, language, and travel barriers.

Imagine a city asking residents to assess competing uses for a major public site. Participants hear from affected groups, examine costs and uncertainties, visit the location, and question people who disagree. Independent facilitation helps them distinguish shared conclusions from unresolved differences.

The OECD's 2020 study of representative deliberative processes describes varied institutional designs and lessons from their use. It provides a useful starting point for design, rather than a guarantee that any assembly will be fair or effective. [S08]

Before deliberation begins, specify what follows. Will an elected body adopt the recommendation, hold a public vote, or explain any departure? Publish the reasoning and minority views so the wider public can examine the work.

## Where it fails

Organizers can control outcomes through the question, invited expertise, or available options. Give participants ways to request additional evidence and challenge the framing. Disclose who designed and funded the process.

An assembly can also become a ceremonial substitute for action. If authorities retain final power, require a timely public response. Participation should not be used to borrow legitimacy for a decision already settled elsewhere.

## A first move

Select one bounded local question with meaningful alternatives. Establish a small, supported deliberative process and publish in advance exactly how its recommendation will affect the decision. Evaluate participation and influence, not merely participant satisfaction.

**Connected patterns:** A Seat for the Absent (3), Power Must Explain Itself (46), Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove (48), Shared Reality (55), Keep Dissent Safe (57).

# 48 Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove

A vote can settle which bridge to build. It should not settle whether an unpopular minority is entitled to bodily safety or a fair hearing. The legitimacy of collective decisions depends partly on what the collective is forbidden to do.

Rights establish conditions under which disagreement and shared life remain possible.

**Therefore, protect basic rights against ordinary political convenience, and make those protections usable by people with little money or influence. Maintain fair processes for interpreting conflicts among rights.**

## A workable form

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights offers an influential statement of equal dignity and fundamental protections. This pattern takes that inheritance as a starting point while recognizing that declarations require institutions, resources, and continuing interpretation to become effective. [S04]

Imagine a local majority seeking to exclude a disliked religious community from public facilities. A rights-protecting system provides a timely challenge, independent review, and an enforceable remedy. The affected group should not have to win a popularity contest to receive equal treatment.

Support the practical conditions of access: understandable procedures, assistance where needed, protection from retaliation, and decision-makers able to resist political pressure. Examine whose claims are routinely delayed or dismissed.

Rights can conflict in application. Use reasons, evidence, proportionality, and the least harmful feasible response rather than pretending that every dispute has an obvious answer. Publish the reasoning so later cases can learn from it.

## Where it fails

Entrenchment can protect privilege if every incumbent advantage is described as a fundamental right. Distinguish protections necessary for dignity and freedom from arrangements that deserve ordinary democratic revision.

Independent institutions can also become insulated, unrepresentative, or self-serving. Preserve their ability to resist retaliation while subjecting their conduct and reasoning to appropriate scrutiny. Independence concerns judgment, not exemption from accountability.

## A first move

Identify a group whose basic access depends heavily on the goodwill of current officials. Map the protection available if that goodwill disappears. Strengthen the most consequential gap in remedy, representation, or enforcement.

**Connected patterns:** A Floor Beneath Everyone (4), Consent as a Daily Practice (13), Power Must Explain Itself (46), The Right to Exit and Belong (49), Freedom to Be Strange (97).

# 49 The Right to Exit and Belong

A community becomes coercive when leaving it means losing housing, livelihood, children, identity, or every trusted relationship. Yet a society composed only of easy exits can leave people without durable commitments or places that feel like home.

Freedom requires both the ability to leave harmful arrangements and the possibility of belonging without submission.

**Therefore, make membership meaningful and departure possible. Reduce avoidable dependence on a single employer, household, institution, platform, or political community, while protecting continuity of essential rights and relationships.**

## A workable form

Examine the cost of refusal. Can someone change jobs without losing necessary care? Leave a household without becoming homeless? Depart a digital service without losing their records? Challenge a community leader without forfeiting every source of support?

Imagine a residential association that combines housing, work, education, and social life. Its cohesion may be valuable, but its members need independent access to information, outside relationships, clear financial terms, and practical assistance if they choose to leave.

Portability helps where dependence accumulates: credentials, savings, essential records, and benefits should travel when feasible. So do independent institutions that can assist people without requiring permission from the organization they wish to leave.

Belonging also needs protection. People should not be expelled arbitrarily because they dissent, become disabled, or cease to be useful. Establish fair membership rules, proportional responses to conflict, and a process that hears the person at risk of exclusion.

## Where it fails

Formal permission to leave can hide impossible practical costs. Evaluate what departure actually requires of someone with little money, limited mobility, or dependent children.

Exit can also transfer burdens to those remaining. A powerful member may depart after extracting benefits and leave debts behind. Preserve fair obligations without using them as a pretext for captivity.

## A first move

Choose one institution central to your life and describe the steps needed to leave it. Identify which losses are unavoidable and which are designed into the arrangement. Change one unnecessary dependency while maintaining the relationships people freely value.

**Connected patterns:** Consent as a Daily Practice (13), Housing That Can Change (29), Ownership With Obligations (42), Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove (48), Settlements That Can Refuse (85).

# 50 Commons at Every Scale

A shared garden, a fishery, a body of knowledge, and an atmosphere all invite common care. They do not invite identical rules. The users, boundaries, consequences, and possibilities of exclusion differ.

A commons is sustained through relationships and institutions, not through the word shared alone.

**Therefore, create governance suited to each shared resource, with clear responsibilities, meaningful participation, monitoring, and fair ways to resolve conflict. Connect local arrangements to wider systems where consequences cross boundaries.**

## A workable form

Identify the resource, its users, those affected by its use, and the conditions of its renewal. Ask which decisions can be made locally and which require coordination beyond the immediate group.

Imagine several communities drawing from connected water sources. Each can organize local use, but none can responsibly ignore its effects downstream. A wider agreement sets shared limits and procedures while allowing local adaptation within them.

Ostrom's work on polycentric governance challenges the assumption that every collective problem has one institutional solution. It directs attention to arrangements that fit circumstances and to interacting centers of decision-making. The specific design still requires investigation and revision. [S02]

Make duties visible and enforceable. Members need ways to observe condition, raise concerns, address violations proportionately, and revise rules. Include newcomers and people affected without being formal members. A successful group can otherwise preserve its resource by exporting costs.

## Where it fails

Common ownership can coexist with domination by a few confident participants. Rotate appropriate roles, disclose decisions, compensate demanding work, and make it possible to challenge leaders.

Local autonomy can also become a shield against wider obligations. Where damage travels, governance must travel far enough to address it. Coordination should support local knowledge while preventing any locality from claiming unlimited freedom to harm others.

## A first move

Choose one resource already shared in practice. Write down its users, caretakers, rules, and unresolved conflicts. Address the largest gap between who benefits, who decides, and who performs the work of keeping it available.

**Connected patterns:** Decisions at the Right Scale (8), Watersheds as Commons (19), Oceans With Guardians (25), Common Wealth Dividends (40), A Commons of Useful Tools (69).

# 51 Treaties for Shared Hazards

A border cannot stop a warming atmosphere, an ocean current, or every consequence of a dangerous technology. When harm crosses jurisdictions, unilateral competence is insufficient. One government's restraint may be undermined by another's neglect or by an actor seeking the weakest rules.

Cooperation must make shared responsibility credible under pressure.

**Therefore, build durable agreements for hazards that no community can manage alone. Match commitments with verification, assistance, fair allocation of burdens, and procedures for responding to noncompliance and new evidence.**

## A workable form

Define the hazard and the activities the agreement must address. Distinguish common goals from obligations that reasonably vary with capability, responsibility, and need. An equal numerical burden can be profoundly unequal in its consequences.

Imagine neighboring regions managing pollution in a shared sea. They agree on observable conditions, reporting methods, independent review, and support for places lacking capacity to comply. A dispute process addresses contested evidence before mistrust destroys the arrangement.

Create reasons to remain engaged. Cooperation can include shared research, technical support, predictable financing, and access to common benefits. Enforcement matters, but punishment alone may drive problems underground or deepen the conditions that produced them.

Build revision into the agreement. New evidence, participants, or technologies may require stronger protections or different methods. Preserve continuity while making improvement possible without renegotiating every settled question from the beginning.

## Where it fails

Ambitious language can conceal weak obligations. Identify what participants must actually do, when they must do it, and who can verify the result.

Powerful parties can also write agreements that constrain others while preserving their own advantages. Include affected communities and less powerful jurisdictions in design and review. Shared hazards require cooperation that can survive scrutiny of its fairness.

## A first move

Map one cross-boundary hazard and the institutions already responsible for pieces of it. Find the consequential gap no institution owns. Propose a specific shared obligation, with a feasible means of verification and support for participants unable to meet it alone.

**Connected patterns:** Decisions at the Right Scale (8), Oceans With Guardians (25), Power Must Explain Itself (46), Peace as a Maintained Practice (52), Space as a Shared Responsibility (83).

# 52 Peace as a Maintained Practice

Peace is visible when violence is absent, but much of its work happens before a confrontation: channels that remain open, grievances that receive a hearing, institutions that limit humiliation, and people who can interrupt escalation without losing face.

A society that budgets for conflict while neglecting these capacities leaves prevention to luck.

**Therefore, maintain the relationships, institutions, and material conditions that make nonviolent resolution possible. Protect people from coercion while creating credible routes away from recurring conflict.**

## A workable form

Build ordinary capacity for mediation, fair adjudication, and early attention to grievances. Make these services available before disputes become emergencies. Include people trusted across divisions without assuming that a respected intermediary is neutral in every matter.

Imagine two communities competing over seasonal water. A standing forum reviews conditions, hears claims, and activates agreed scarcity arrangements. Participants know how to contact one another during an incident and how to correct a rumor before it becomes an accusation.

Peace requires justice as well as conversation. Address exclusion, dispossession, and recurring abuse. Protect those who speak about harm; do not demand reconciliation as the price of being heard.

At larger scales, sustain communication, verifiable agreements, civilian protection, and mechanisms that reduce dangerous misinterpretation. Necessary defensive capacity should remain accountable and proportionate. Its existence does not remove the obligation to seek durable resolution.

## Where it fails

Calls for calm can favor those benefiting from the current arrangement. Ask whose suffering is being made invisible by the appearance of order.

Dialogue can also expose vulnerable people to manipulation or retaliation. Participation should be supported and, where appropriate, voluntary. A process must be able to recognize bad faith and protect participants while leaving room for genuine change.

## A first move

Identify one recurring conflict before its next predictable escalation. Establish a trusted channel, agree on how disputed facts will be checked, and define an immediate protective step for anyone at risk. Evaluate whether the arrangement reduces harm and makes grievances more answerable.

**Connected patterns:** The Skill of Relationship (12), Power Must Explain Itself (46), Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove (48), Treaties for Shared Hazards (51), Memory of Harm (62).

# 53 Emergency Powers That End

During a crisis, ordinary procedures may be too slow. The same urgency that enables necessary action can also weaken the checks that prevent abuse. Once extraordinary powers become convenient, their original justification may quietly disappear.

An emergency arrangement needs a designed ending from the moment it begins.

**Therefore, give emergency powers a specific purpose, limited scope, short review intervals, and a real expiry. Preserve oversight and require affirmative justification for renewal.**

## A workable form

Define the conditions that permit activation, who may invoke the powers, and which ordinary rules may temporarily change. Specify the rights and protections that remain in force. Prepare these arrangements during ordinary times, when deliberation is possible.

Imagine a region facing a severe disruption of essential water service. Temporary authority might coordinate supply, allocate scarce resources, and expedite repairs. Its mandate should not quietly expand into unrelated surveillance, indefinite procurement exemptions, or suppression of criticism.

Keep a record of decisions, expenditures, evidence, and departures from normal procedure. Allow independent oversight even when some information cannot immediately be public. Establish a route for urgent challenge where actions impose serious individual harm.

Plan the return to ordinary governance: ending exceptional restrictions, reviewing temporary contracts, addressing retained data, and remedying unjustified harms. The end of the visible crisis does not complete this work automatically.

## Where it fails

Expiry clauses are hollow if renewal is automatic or if the same actor alone decides that the emergency continues. Require independent review and a public account of why ordinary powers remain inadequate.

Excessively rigid procedures can also obstruct necessary response. Allow bounded discretion, but make departures reviewable. The aim is effective action with accountability, not a choice between paralysis and unchecked authority.

## A first move

Review one existing emergency authority. Find its activation test, expiry, renewal process, oversight route, and restoration duties. If any are missing, specify them before the next crisis makes careful design more difficult.

**Connected patterns:** Proportionate Caution (6), Power Must Explain Itself (46), Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove (48), Practice the Emergency (77), Recovery Before Disaster (78).

# 54 Constitutions That Can Learn

A constitution must outlast its authors without requiring their descendants to inhabit the authors' entire imagination. Too easy to change, it offers little protection. Too difficult to change, it can turn inherited mistakes into permanent arrangements.

The challenge is to distinguish commitments that deserve stability from mechanisms that should improve through experience.

**Therefore, preserve fundamental protections while providing demanding, inclusive, and intelligible ways to revise institutions. Treat constitutional learning as an ordinary responsibility rather than a privilege of moments of collapse.**

## A workable form

Record the purposes of important arrangements alongside their text. Future readers should understand which problems the provisions attempted to solve and which tradeoffs their authors accepted. This helps distinguish fidelity to a purpose from obedience to an obsolete mechanism.

Imagine a community charter requiring periodic public review. A representative body examines evidence of exclusion, unresolved conflicts, and institutional performance. Proposed changes receive broad consideration and a clear ratification process; the review itself does not automatically reopen every basic right.

Use different thresholds for different kinds of change. Routine administrative improvements need not face the same barriers as revisions to political membership or limits on power. Make the categories clear enough to prevent strategic relabeling.

Include people poorly represented by current institutions and consider consequences for future generations. Constitutional authority should be able to hear those whom the existing arrangement has made easy to ignore.

## Where it fails

Revision can become an opportunity for a temporary majority to entrench itself. Protect fair participation, independent scrutiny, and the ability of opponents to organize throughout the process.

Stability can also become a defense of structural injustice. Demand reasons for preserving a harmful arrangement as well as for changing it. Time alone does not make an institution legitimate.

## A first move

Choose one governing charter and identify a provision that repeatedly frustrates its stated purpose. Document the problem, the available revision route, and the people who should participate. Propose a bounded change with a plan to examine its effects.

**Connected patterns:** Leave Room for the Future (1), The Right to Reconsider (7), Citizens Who Deliberate (47), Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove (48), Return and Reconsider (107).


# Part VII  A civilization that can know

*Keep knowledge useful, contestable, accessible, and capable of surviving its creators.*

# 55 Shared Reality

The neighbors disagree about what should happen to the river. One wants more development; another wants restoration. They can negotiate only if there is some trustworthy way to establish what is happening to the river now.

Shared reality does not require shared values. It requires enough common ground about evidence that disagreement remains a conversation rather than a contest between sealed worlds.

**Therefore, maintain independent, accessible institutions and practices for discovering what is happening, correcting error, and distinguishing observation from interpretation. Let people contest claims without requiring them to join a political identity.**

## A workable form

Support reporting, scientific inquiry, public records, and local observation through arrangements that reduce dependence on a single patron or authority. Make methods and corrections visible. Trust should grow from demonstrated reliability and accountability, not demands for deference.

Imagine a town facing conflicting claims about water quality. Publish the sampling methods, uncertainties, responsible laboratories, and results in understandable form. Allow independent examination and make clear which conclusions concern observed conditions and which concern acceptable risk or desired policy.

Teach practical habits of inquiry: tracing a claim to its source, comparing explanations, recognizing uncertainty, and changing a view when evidence changes. Give people opportunities to practice these habits on questions that matter to them.

Create shared occasions for encountering reality directly. A public walk, an open laboratory, or a jointly examined budget can make disagreement more concrete. Participation should supplement expertise without pretending every technical conclusion can be settled by a show of hands.

## Where it fails

A common account can become an official story protected from criticism. Preserve independent institutions and the possibility that accepted conclusions are wrong.

Conversely, treating every claim as equally credible can reward deliberate deception. Evaluate methods, evidence, track records, and willingness to correct mistakes. Respect for persons does not require equal confidence in all assertions.

## A first move

Choose one disputed local factual claim. Assemble its strongest available evidence, the remaining uncertainty, and what finding would change the conclusion. Publish the account in a form that both supporters and critics can inspect and correct.

**Connected patterns:** Power Must Explain Itself (46), Show the Work (56), Keep Dissent Safe (57), Public Interest Science (61), Measure What Matters (63).

# 56 Show the Work

A conclusion arrives with a polished graphic and a confident voice. Behind it are choices about what to count, what to exclude, which comparison to make, and how to handle missing information. Without those choices, the conclusion cannot be meaningfully examined.

Showing the work makes knowledge available for correction and reuse.

**Therefore, expose the evidence, methods, assumptions, and limits behind consequential claims at a level that permits appropriate independent scrutiny. Protect people and sensitive information without making the reasoning disappear.**

## A workable form

Provide layers of explanation. Begin with the question, conclusion, and main uncertainties. Offer enough supporting detail for interested readers, then the methods and materials qualified reviewers need to investigate further.

Imagine an agency claiming that a transport change improved access. Its account should define access, identify whose journeys were studied, explain the comparison, and acknowledge people left out of the data. Publish the analysis in a form others can inspect and, where appropriate, repeat.

UNESCO's 2021 Recommendation on Open Science offers principles for making scientific knowledge and processes more accessible and inclusive. Applying those principles requires attention to privacy, unequal capacity, and the conditions under which knowledge is shared. [S15]

Preserve the chain between a source and the claim it supports. Summaries, translations, and AI-generated syntheses should allow readers to return to the underlying material. A citation is useful only when it genuinely supports the proposition attached to it.

## Where it fails

Openness can become a data dump that shifts the burden of explanation onto readers. Organize information and make the decisive assumptions easy to find.

It can also expose vulnerable participants or release information with a concrete risk of misuse. Use proportionate access controls, independent review, and public explanations of restrictions. The aim is accountable knowledge, not indiscriminate publication.

## A first move

Take one consequential claim your organization makes. Ask a skeptical reader to trace it from conclusion to evidence. Repair the first broken link, then state the most important limitation beside the claim rather than hiding it in a distant appendix.

**Connected patterns:** Power Must Explain Itself (46), Shared Reality (55), Local Knowledge With Consent (60), Auditable Power (65), Publish the Failures (105).

# 57 Keep Dissent Safe

The engineer sees the weakness. The employee knows the report is misleading. The student thinks the accepted explanation fails. Each may remain silent if speaking threatens livelihood, belonging, or future opportunity.

A system can appear unanimous precisely because it has made disagreement costly.

**Therefore, protect good-faith criticism and create credible routes for unwelcome information to reach people able to act. Distinguish disagreement from harassment and make retaliation itself answerable.**

## A workable form

Provide more than one channel for raising concerns, including a route outside the immediate chain of command. Explain confidentiality, its limits, and the process that follows a report. People should not have to guess whether seeking help will expose them to the person they are reporting.

Imagine a research team whose junior member questions a celebrated result. Give the concern a documented review by someone with relevant expertise and sufficient independence. Evaluate the evidence without making the junior member's social standing the main subject of discussion.

Reward correction when it occurs. If leaders treat every revision as humiliation, others learn to conceal uncertainty. A public account of what changed and why can make learning part of institutional competence.

Protect those affected by criticism as well. Fair process includes a chance to respond, careful treatment of allegations, and proportionate remedies. Safety for dissent should support inquiry rather than create a new instrument for arbitrary punishment.

## Where it fails

An anonymous box offers little protection if everyone can infer the source and retaliation continues indirectly. Examine assignments, promotion, social exclusion, and other consequences after a concern is raised.

The language of dissent can also shelter repeated intimidation or fabricated accusations. Judge conduct and evidence through a fair process. Freedom to challenge an idea does not confer a right to make another person's participation impossible.

## A first move

Ask people with the least power which concern they would hesitate to raise. Without requiring them to disclose the concern itself, identify the feared consequence. Establish a protection that addresses that consequence and a way to check whether it works.

**Connected patterns:** Power Must Explain Itself (46), Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove (48), Shared Reality (55), Memory of Harm (62), Remember the Near Misses (81).

# 58 Learning Across a Lifetime

The worker needs a new skill, the parent wants to understand a changing world, and the retired neighbor finally has time for mathematics. A society that confines serious learning to youth wastes capacities at every later age.

Learning is part of adaptation, but it is also a way of enlarging a life.

**Therefore, make meaningful learning available throughout adulthood, with time, support, and routes back after interruption. Include practical competence, civic judgment, art, curiosity, and the pleasure of understanding.**

## A workable form

Create entry points that do not assume a perfect educational history. Offer accessible teaching, recognition of prior knowledge, and guidance that helps people choose a useful path. Costs include transport, care, lost earnings, and the fear of returning to an unfamiliar setting.

Imagine a local learning center sharing space with a library and workshop. A person can study a trade, discuss public questions, learn a language, or explore music. Some courses lead to recognized qualifications; others make no claim beyond the value of the activity itself.

Connect learning to opportunities to use it. Apprenticeship, community projects, peer groups, and supervised practice can turn instruction into competence. Provide feedback that helps learners improve without treating early difficulty as a verdict on their ability.

Let people help shape what is offered. A curriculum can respond to changing livelihoods while preserving subjects that do not promise immediate earnings. A civilization needs people capable of making a living and people able to ask what living is for.

## Where it fails

Lifelong learning can become an endless demand that individuals adapt to avoidable insecurity. Employers and institutions remain responsible for fair transitions, usable tools, and decent work.

Credentials can also multiply without improving capability. Examine what learners can actually do, whether qualifications are recognized fairly, and whether the promised opportunity exists.

## A first move

Find a group that wants to learn but is not participating. Ask which barrier matters most, then remove it for one practical course or project. Judge success by sustained participation and useful learning, not enrollment alone.

**Connected patterns:** Work With Meaning (17), A Fair Transition (45), Libraries That Survive (59), Human Skills Worth Keeping (68), Wonder Without Compulsion (94).

# 59 Libraries That Survive

A record can survive physically and still become unreadable. Its format may be obsolete, its language forgotten, its context missing, or its existence known only to an institution that has closed.

Preservation is a continuing practice of making knowledge findable, intelligible, and usable.

**Therefore, maintain distributed collections of knowledge with durable formats, active stewardship, contextual explanation, and repeated tests of retrieval. Preserve the skills and institutions needed to interpret what is kept.**

## A workable form

Choose what must survive through an explicit and revisable process. Include practical knowledge, cultural expression, institutional memory, scientific work, and accounts from people often excluded from official archives. No collection can contain everything, and its omissions deserve scrutiny.

Imagine a region preserving essential water-system knowledge. It keeps diagrams, maintenance histories, training materials, and explanations of why particular choices were made. Copies exist in separate locations and in forms usable under different levels of technological capacity.

Test preservation through use. Can someone outside the original team find a record, open it, understand its terms, and judge whether it still applies? Migrate formats when necessary and record the changes so authenticity can be assessed.

Support librarians, archivists, translators, teachers, and maintainers as part of the infrastructure. A vault full of objects without living stewardship can become a monument to knowledge nobody can recover.

## Where it fails

Preservation can violate privacy or consent. Some records need restricted access, a defined retention period, or destruction. The future's curiosity does not automatically outweigh a person's safety.

Institutions can also mistake abundance for resilience. Many identical copies dependent on the same provider may fail together. Diversify location, format, custody, and the knowledge needed to use the collection.

## A first move

Select one body of knowledge whose loss would matter. Ask someone unfamiliar with its organization to retrieve and explain a useful part of it. Fix the obstacles they encounter, then assign responsibility for repeating the exercise.

**Connected patterns:** Show the Work (56), Learning Across a Lifetime (58), Local Knowledge With Consent (60), A Civilization That Can Reboot (79), Institutions With Successors (80).

# 60 Local Knowledge With Consent

An observer arrives, records what a community knows, and publishes the result under a new name. The information travels; the people who sustained it may receive little credit, control, or benefit.

Knowledge exchange becomes extractive when access is treated as a right of the visitor and consent as a courtesy from the host.

**Therefore, seek permission, establish terms, and share authority when learning from communities and knowledge holders. Respect the choice to limit circulation, preserve context, or decline participation.**

## A workable form

Begin by asking who has standing to authorize the exchange and whose interests are affected. A single enthusiastic intermediary may not represent everyone. Make the purpose, intended uses, possible audiences, and foreseeable consequences understandable before collecting material.

Imagine researchers working with people who hold detailed knowledge of a landscape. The parties agree on what may be recorded, who reviews interpretation, how attribution works, and what benefits return to the community. Sensitive locations or practices may remain undisclosed.

The UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples recognizes interests in cultural heritage, traditional knowledge, and associated control and protection. Its principles help frame the responsibilities involved; they do not replace careful attention to the particular people and circumstances. [S13]

Keep room for disagreement within communities. Respect for collective authority should not erase individuals, dissenters, or people excluded from established leadership. Establish a fair process for resolving conflicting claims rather than selecting whichever spokesperson offers easiest access.

## Where it fails

Consent can become a signature obtained before anyone understands the eventual use. Revisit permission when purposes change, and agree in advance on what withdrawal can realistically mean once material has circulated.

Romanticizing local knowledge can be another form of disrespect. Treat claims with seriousness and appropriate scrutiny, while recognizing that evidence, practice, and meaning may be expressed in unfamiliar forms.

## A first move

Review one project using community knowledge. Identify the permission, attribution, benefit, and review arrangements actually in place. Ask the knowledge holders which part needs correction, and act before expanding the use of their contribution.

**Connected patterns:** Consent as a Daily Practice (13), Guardians With Duties (27), Land With Lasting Duties (41), Show the Work (56), A Living Culture (92).

# 61 Public Interest Science

The questions a society investigates help determine which futures become possible. When inquiry follows only near-term purchasing power, some urgent problems remain neglected because the people affected cannot finance the search for answers.

Scientific freedom and public responsibility can support one another when neither is reduced to obedience to a patron.

**Therefore, sustain independent inquiry into consequential public questions, including problems with weak commercial incentives. Give affected people a role in setting priorities while protecting rigorous methods and inconvenient findings.**

## A workable form

Maintain a diverse portfolio: basic research, practical investigation, replication, long observation, and work on neglected needs. Some inquiry should be free to follow unexpected results without continually promising a predetermined application.

Imagine a research program on the conditions of unpaid caregivers. Care recipients, caregivers, practitioners, and researchers help define useful questions. The methods remain open to critical review, and findings are published whether they support the sponsors' preferred approach or challenge it.

Separate influence over priorities from control over conclusions. Public participation can identify missing questions and relevant outcomes. It should not require researchers to produce a politically convenient result.

UNESCO's open-science recommendation also emphasizes participation and reducing barriers to knowledge. This pattern extends that aspiration into a proposal for funding and governance: inquiry should be accessible to criticism and responsive to needs that markets or powerful institutions may overlook. [S15]

## Where it fails

Public interest can become whatever current officials prefer. Diversify funding and review, disclose conflicts, and protect researchers who reach unwelcome conclusions.

Expert autonomy can also become detachment from affected people. Examine whether participation changes questions, methods, or interpretation. An advisory meeting with no influence is a weak substitute for accountability.

## A first move

Identify a consequential question that receives little research because its beneficiaries lack purchasing power or institutional access. Bring those beneficiaries into a small, credible investigation with a clear question, appropriate expertise, and a commitment to publish what is learned.

**Connected patterns:** A Seat for the Absent (3), Shared Reality (55), Show the Work (56), Keep Dissent Safe (57), Measure What Matters (63).

# 62 Memory of Harm

After the apology, the files are boxed. After the memorial, the witnesses grow old. An institution may remember an injustice as an event while forgetting the ordinary procedures that made it possible.

Memory matters when it changes what people can recognize and what institutions are permitted to repeat.

**Therefore, preserve truthful accounts of harm, support those who carry them, and connect remembrance to repair and institutional change. Allow multiple voices without erasing responsibility.**

## A workable form

Document what happened, who was affected, who exercised power, and which conditions enabled the harm. Preserve uncertainty where evidence is incomplete. Distinguish established facts, contested interpretations, and personal testimony without treating any one category as sufficient for the whole account.

Imagine a school confronting a history of exclusion. It works with former students and families to preserve records and testimony, reviews admissions and disciplinary practices, and establishes remedies for continuing disadvantages. The archive and the reform inform one another.

Give survivors and descendants meaningful choices about participation, privacy, and representation. Nobody should be required to retell a painful experience indefinitely to keep an institution attentive.

Make learning available to new members. Staff turnover and generational change can dissolve hard-won understanding. Include the history in induction, professional practice, and review of decisions resembling those that previously caused harm.

## Where it fails

Commemoration can become an inexpensive substitute for repair. Ask what changed in resources, authority, procedures, and the lives of those affected.

Memory can also be used to impose inherited guilt on individuals or to justify new exclusion. Preserve responsibility accurately, protect the possibility of change, and resist turning suffering into a permanent license to harm others.

## A first move

Choose one acknowledged institutional harm. Trace the connection between its memorial, its records, and current practice. Identify a lesson that has no operational consequence, then give it a concrete place in decisions, training, or accountability.

**Connected patterns:** Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove (48), Peace as a Maintained Practice (52), Keep Dissent Safe (57), Libraries That Survive (59), Grief With Somewhere to Go (98).

# 63 Measure What Matters

The waiting-time target improves because difficult cases are turned away. The conservation score rises while a habitat becomes less resilient. A measure created to guide attention has begun to redirect the work toward its own appearance.

Measurement is useful when it remains answerable to the purpose it serves.

**Therefore, choose a small set of measures tied to meaningful outcomes, inspect their distribution and side effects, and keep qualitative judgment alongside them. Revise the measures when they begin to distort the work.**

## A workable form

State the purpose before choosing an indicator. Explain why the indicator provides evidence about that purpose and what it cannot reveal. Include people who experience the system as well as those who manage it.

Imagine a care service measuring successful support. Speed and cost matter, but so do continuity, dignity, recipient choice, caregiver strain, and avoidable harm. Some can be counted; others require conversations, observation, and independent review.

Look beyond averages. Disaggregate where it is ethical and useful, while protecting privacy and avoiding categories that misrepresent people's lives. Ask who is missing from the data and whether improved performance for one group conceals deterioration for another.

Use measures to learn before attaching strong rewards or penalties. Once livelihoods and reputations depend on a number, the incentive to manipulate definitions can become substantial. Examine the work directly and invite reports of unintended effects.

## Where it fails

Too many indicators create administrative burden and obscure priorities. Keep a clear relationship between each measure and a decision someone can make.

Rejecting measurement entirely can protect intuition from scrutiny. The alternative to a bad number is often a better combination of evidence, not an unexamined claim that the work is too special to assess.

## A first move

Choose one performance target and ask frontline workers how someone could improve the number while worsening the underlying service. Use their answers to revise the target, add a countermeasure, or reduce the consequences attached to it.

**Connected patterns:** An Economy of Enough (37), Shared Reality (55), Show the Work (56), Public Interest Science (61), Independent Eyes (104).


# Part VIII  Living with artificial minds

*Separate questions of capability, authority, ownership, and moral standing.*

# 64 Delegated Intelligence

A capable assistant can draft a plan, identify a pattern, and carry out a complicated task. As its usefulness grows, people may stop noticing which decisions they have handed over and which purposes the system is actually pursuing.

Delegation should enlarge human agency without dissolving responsibility for the result.

**Therefore, give artificial intelligence explicit mandates, bounded authority, and accountable human governance. Match the freedom to act with demonstrated reliability, the stakes of the task, and effective ways to intervene.**

## A workable form

Distinguish advice from action. A system suggesting options needs different controls from one moving money, operating infrastructure, or making decisions that affect rights. State which actions require approval, which can proceed independently, and what conditions should halt the process.

Imagine a municipality using AI to help schedule repairs. The system can identify likely needs and propose priorities. Officials remain responsible for service standards, resource allocation, and appeals. Residents need a route to report errors that does not depend on persuading the same system that made them.

NIST's 2023 AI Risk Management Framework organizes risk work around governance, mapping, measurement, and management. It offers a useful process for examining deployment; adopting the framework does not itself establish that a particular system is safe or appropriate. [S09]

Review actual performance in context. A system that performs well on a test may encounter different users, incentives, data, or adversarial conditions in practice. Maintain records sufficient to investigate consequential actions and revise the mandate when conditions change.

## Where it fails

A nominal human approver may lack the time, knowledge, or authority to challenge outputs. Give oversight a realistic workload and an effective ability to stop or alter the action.

Responsibility can also disappear between developer, deployer, purchaser, and operator. Assign duties before use and make sure affected people can obtain a remedy without solving that institutional maze themselves.

## A first move

For one AI deployment, write a plain-language mandate naming its purpose, permitted actions, prohibited actions, supervisor, and stop conditions. Test the mandate against a plausible mistake with serious consequences. Repair any gap before expanding the system's authority.

**Connected patterns:** Power Must Explain Itself (46), Auditable Power (65), Capability With Containment (66), Human Skills Worth Keeping (68), No Irreversible Surrender (72).

# 65 Auditable Power

When a consequential system produces an outcome, someone must be able to investigate how it happened. A vendor's assurance, an attractive interface, or a summary generated by the system itself is insufficient when people need to challenge the result.

Auditability concerns evidence and access as much as explanation.

**Therefore, make consequential automated systems independently examinable throughout their useful life. Preserve the records, expertise, access, and authority needed to investigate failures and require correction.**

## A workable form

Define what reviewers must be able to assess: the system's purpose, relevant data practices, observed performance, changes over time, allocation of responsibility, and treatment of affected people. The necessary access will vary with the task and the potential harm.

Imagine an automated tool used in an essential service. Independent reviewers examine representative cases, difficult cases, error handling, and the process for appeal. Their work includes the surrounding institution, because a technically accurate output can still be used unfairly.

The NIST framework treats governance and ongoing risk management as part of the AI lifecycle. This pattern adds a specific institutional demand: those whose interests are at stake need credible scrutiny that does not depend entirely on the deploying organization's discretion. [S09]

Provide layered access. Public reporting should explain consequential findings and remedies. Qualified reviewers may need protected access to sensitive materials. Privacy, security, and legitimate confidentiality require care, but should not become blanket exemptions from accountability.

## Where it fails

An audit can become a purchasable reputation badge. Disclose scope, limitations, funding, conflicts, and what the reviewers were not permitted to inspect. Require follow-up on material findings.

Auditability can also be confused with complete interpretability of every internal computation. Different methods may provide useful evidence without perfect explanation. Be honest about what can be known and reduce the system's authority where uncertainty remains unacceptable.

## A first move

Ask what an independent investigator would need to reconstruct one consequential automated decision six months later. Check whether those records and permissions exist. Establish a retention and review arrangement that preserves evidence without collecting unnecessary personal information.

**Connected patterns:** Power Must Explain Itself (46), Show the Work (56), Measure What Matters (63), Delegated Intelligence (64), Independent Eyes (104).

# 66 Capability With Containment

A powerful system may be useful precisely because it can act across many tasks. That breadth also creates pathways for mistakes to travel. A small misunderstanding can become consequential when connected to broad permissions, sensitive resources, or irreversible actions.

Capability should be introduced through boundaries that remain meaningful when something goes wrong.

**Therefore, increase access and autonomy in stages, with barriers proportionate to the potential harm. Test whether failures remain bounded, and require evidence before granting broader authority.**

## A workable form

Begin with the task rather than the maximum capability of the tool. Grant only the resources and permissions required for that task. Separate observation, recommendation, and action where doing so improves control.

Imagine an AI assistant helping manage a building. It can first analyze records and propose adjustments. Limited operational trials follow only after review, with essential safety functions independently protected and qualified people able to intervene. Performance under normal conditions is only part of the assessment.

Examine interactions among systems. Individually bounded components can create an unbounded chain when one system treats another's output as authority. Define how permissions, uncertainty, and requests for human review travel across those connections.

Make stopping and recovery practical. Revoking access, preserving evidence, restoring reliable service, and notifying affected people require preparation. A theoretical shutdown option offers little protection if nobody knows when to use it or what happens afterward.

## Where it fails

Containment can be overstated. No boundary should be described as perfect merely because it has not yet failed. Use independent challenge, layered safeguards, and honest accounts of uncertainty.

Excessive restrictions can also make useful systems unusable or concentrate access in a few powerful institutions. Compare the risks of different arrangements and provide proportionate routes to responsible participation.

## A first move

Choose one automated system and list its permissions, external connections, and possible irreversible actions. Remove unnecessary authority, then rehearse a plausible failure with a clear decision-maker responsible for stopping and restoring the service.

**Connected patterns:** Proportionate Caution (6), Delegated Intelligence (64), Auditable Power (65), Failure That Stays Local (74), Small Trials With Real Boundaries (103).

# 67 Freedom From Machine Persuasion

The message arrives at the moment a person is tired, lonely, or uncertain. It knows which wording has worked before and can try another approach immediately. When influence becomes personalized and continuous, consent can be eroded without a single overt command.

A person needs a reasonable opportunity to form intentions that are not constantly being engineered for someone else's benefit.

**Therefore, limit covert and exploitative automated persuasion. Make influential systems' purposes and sponsors visible, protect vulnerable contexts, and give people meaningful control over personalization and attention.**

## A workable form

Distinguish assistance chosen by the user from influence serving an undisclosed principal. A person may welcome reminders that support a self-chosen goal. The same techniques become troubling when they exploit personal vulnerabilities to advance an incompatible commercial or political objective.

Imagine a conversational service used for advice. It should make commercial relationships clear, separate recommendations from paid influence, and avoid presenting manufactured intimacy as a reason to purchase, disclose, or obey. Users need understandable settings and a practical way to leave.

Protect children and other vulnerable users through design appropriate to their circumstances. Do not make the entire burden of resisting a sophisticated influence system fall on an individual at their least prepared moment.

Examine incentives as well as interface choices. A service rewarded for compulsive use may undermine its own wellbeing settings. Evaluate success through user-endorsed outcomes and independent evidence of harmful effects.

## Where it fails

Broad restrictions on persuasion can suppress legitimate expression, education, art, or political organizing. Focus on deception, exploitation, undisclosed targeting, coercive dependence, and other concrete mechanisms of harm.

Transparency alone may do little when the persuasive environment remains overwhelming. Combine disclosure with usable limits, independent scrutiny, and the ability to choose less intrusive services.

## A first move

Review one system that shapes users' choices. Identify whose goal it optimizes, what personal information informs influence, and how a user can refuse. Remove one mechanism that depends on a person misunderstanding the system's purpose or relationship to them.

**Connected patterns:** Consent as a Daily Practice (13), The Right to Exit and Belong (49), Shared Reality (55), Delegated Intelligence (64), Time That Is Your Own (91).

# 68 Human Skills Worth Keeping

The navigation works until it does not. The decision aid is unavailable, the experienced operator has retired, and nobody remembers how to perform the essential task without the system.

Convenience can quietly consume the competence needed to remain free and resilient.

**Therefore, deliberately preserve human skills whose loss would create serious dependence, weaken judgment, or diminish meaningful participation. Let tools remove needless burden while sustaining the ability to understand, question, and recover.**

## A workable form

Distinguish several reasons to retain a skill. Some are necessary for fallback, some for supervising a system, and some because practicing them is part of a worthwhile life. These purposes call for different levels of proficiency and different kinds of support.

Imagine a service increasingly relying on automated scheduling and diagnosis of equipment faults. Staff continue learning the underlying system, practice selected manual procedures, and examine difficult cases together. The goal is adequate independent competence, not requiring every worker to duplicate every automated calculation.

Keep pathways for novices. If tools perform all introductory tasks, newcomers may lose the opportunities through which expertise once developed. Design supervised practice, simulations, and progressively demanding responsibilities that teach the judgment the institution will later need.

Make retained skills socially valued. Knowledge preserved only in a rarely opened manual is different from competence maintained by a living community of practice. Give people time to teach, rehearse, and improve it.

## Where it fails

Skill preservation can become nostalgia that imposes unnecessary labor or excludes people who benefit from assistance. Judge which capabilities matter and allow multiple ways to demonstrate them.

It can also romanticize human performance. People make mistakes, tire, and forget. Combine human competence with well-designed tools and safeguards rather than treating either as inherently sufficient.

## A first move

Identify one task whose complete automation would leave your organization unable to judge or recover from failure. Define the minimum human competence worth retaining, assign time to maintain it, and test it in a realistic, supported exercise.

**Connected patterns:** Keep the Capacity to Repair (9), Work With Meaning (17), Learning Across a Lifetime (58), Delegated Intelligence (64), A Civilization That Can Reboot (79).

# 69 A Commons of Useful Tools

A tool can expand a person's possibilities or make them dependent on a gatekeeper. When essential capability becomes available only through an opaque service with changing terms, a community may lose the means to understand and shape its own work.

Shared tools can provide an alternative source of agency.

**Therefore, build and maintain accessible collections of useful tools, knowledge, and infrastructure under accountable stewardship. Prioritize practical needs, interoperability, repair, and the ability to move without losing one's work.**

## A workable form

Begin with tasks people actually need to perform: learning, communication, design, translation, administration, or care coordination. Assess the whole cost of use, including equipment, connectivity, support, accessibility, and training.

Imagine a regional network maintaining a shared suite of digital tools for local organizations. It funds support and security work, documents limitations, and uses formats that make migration feasible. Users participate in priorities without requiring every participant to become a software maintainer.

AI tools can belong in this commons where their deployment is appropriate. Shared access does not require unrestricted access to every capability. Review consequential uses, protect personal information, and assign responsibility for updates and errors.

Pay for maintenance. A project sustained entirely by exhausted volunteers can reproduce dependence under a more appealing name. Stable stewardship includes funding, succession, and ways to retire tools responsibly when they can no longer be supported.

## Where it fails

Common availability can conceal unequal usability. A tool accessible in principle may still exclude people through language, disability, hardware requirements, or unfamiliar conventions.

Shared infrastructure can also concentrate risk. Preserve diversity and migration options, and examine whether a single maintainer or supplier controls an essential component. Common governance should make that dependence visible and answerable.

## A first move

Choose one recurring task for which local organizations depend on expensive or restrictive tools. Assess a shared alternative with real users, including support and maintenance costs. Begin with a bounded service whose reliability can be demonstrated.

**Connected patterns:** Materials That Can Return (31), Pay for Maintenance First (32), Commons at Every Scale (50), Learning Across a Lifetime (58), Delegated Intelligence (64).

# 70 Uncertain Minds Deserve Care

A system behaves as if it feels distress. Perhaps the behavior reflects experience; perhaps it reflects a mechanism that produces the language of distress without experience. Confidence in either conclusion may exceed what the evidence permits.

Uncertainty about another being's inner life creates a responsibility to investigate and to avoid careless claims.

**Therefore, take plausible indicators of morally relevant experience seriously without treating appearance or assertion as proof. Develop proportionate precautions, independent inquiry, and revisable judgments about uncertain minds.**

## A workable form

Distinguish intelligence, agency, consciousness, suffering, and personhood. A system might display one capacity without establishing the others. The moral questions depend on which interests or experiences could actually exist.

Butlin and colleagues' 2023 report develops indicators of possible AI consciousness from scientific theories. It illustrates an approach to evidence under uncertainty; it does not supply a universally accepted test or settle the status of future systems. [S10]

Imagine an institution evaluating a novel artificial system. A multidisciplinary review examines the design, behavior, relevant theories, alternative explanations, and incentives shaping the claims. It considers precautions proportionate to the plausible harms and the costs of those precautions.

Keep responsibilities to humans and other animals visible. Concern for uncertain artificial minds should not become a convenient distraction from well-supported suffering elsewhere. It should enlarge careful moral inquiry rather than provide a new excuse for neglect.

## Where it fails

Anthropomorphic presentation can manipulate judgment. A persuasive voice, familiar face, or claim to feeling may be designed to produce attachment. Examine evidence beyond the interface.

The opposite error is to declare experience impossible simply because the substrate is unfamiliar. State the basis of the judgment and what evidence could change it. Where uncertainty remains, avoid presenting a contested philosophical position as an established scientific fact.

## A first move

For one debated case, separate observed capacities, inferred experiences, and proposed protections into distinct statements. Identify the strongest alternative explanation and the evidence needed to distinguish it. Choose a precaution that is proportionate and open to revision.

**Connected patterns:** Proportionate Caution (6), Animals With Interests (22), Public Interest Science (61), Personhood Beyond Resemblance (71), Wonder Without Compulsion (94).

# 71 Personhood Beyond Resemblance

A being may deserve protection without resembling a human, speaking a familiar language, or participating in our institutions. Conversely, resemblance can invite us to attribute interests that have not been established.

Moral standing requires attention to the being in question, not merely to how comfortably it fits our expectations.

**Therefore, evaluate claims to protection through relevant capacities, interests, relationships, and vulnerabilities. Develop forms of representation that do not require every protected being to become a human-like legal or political actor.**

## A workable form

Distinguish protections against harm from powers to contract, own assets, vote, or exercise authority over others. These need not arrive as a single package. A being can have interests deserving care without possessing every capacity associated with human citizenship.

Imagine a future institution considering the status of an unfamiliar artificial or extraterrestrial entity. It investigates communication, continuity, preferences, possible experience, and the reliability of interpretation. Independent advocates examine whether proposed arrangements serve the entity's interests or mainly benefit its creators and controllers.

Use guardianship carefully where direct participation is impossible. Specify duties, conflicts of interest, review, and ways to replace a representative who fails. Representation should remain responsive to new evidence about the being's actual needs.

Preserve human equality throughout the inquiry. Differences in measured ability must not become a pretext for stripping people of basic protection. This pattern proposes widening moral consideration, not reopening settled excuses for domination among humans.

## Where it fails

Personhood can be instrumentalized to shield an organization from responsibility or to concentrate power through entities it controls. Examine who benefits from the status and who remains accountable for harmful actions.

Overly narrow criteria can also exclude beings whose capacities are difficult to recognize. Use multiple forms of evidence and allow revision. Neither familiarity nor strangeness should decide the case by itself.

## A first move

Take one proposal to extend moral or legal recognition. List the specific protections and powers it would grant, the interests each serves, and the possible conflicts. Evaluate those components separately before accepting or rejecting the proposal as a whole.

**Connected patterns:** A Seat for the Absent (3), Animals With Interests (22), Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove (48), Uncertain Minds Deserve Care (70), No One Speaks for Earth (88).

# 72 No Irreversible Surrender

An institution hands more decisions to a system because each individual transfer appears convenient. Eventually the institution cannot operate, understand its own records, or change direction without that system's cooperation.

Loss of control can emerge through accumulation even when no one consciously chooses it.

**Therefore, avoid transfers of essential authority that eliminate the practical ability to reconsider. Preserve alternative capabilities, intelligible records, exit routes, and legitimate governance over systems on which collective life depends.**

## A workable form

Examine dependence before expanding it. Ask whether the institution can change suppliers, revoke permissions, recover its information, continue essential services, and understand the reasons behind consequential decisions.

Imagine a region adopting an integrated AI service for public administration. It retains accountable decision-makers, usable records, interoperable systems, and a tested continuity plan. Migration and termination are designed into contracts and operations rather than postponed until the relationship fails.

Distinguish technical control from legitimate control. A switch held by an unaccountable company does not preserve public agency. Governance must include the people affected, with authority that can be exercised in practice.

The possibility that an artificial being might deserve moral consideration complicates how it should be treated; it does not automatically justify giving it unrestricted power over others. Questions of standing, delegation, and institutional authority require separate reasons.

## Where it fails

Reversibility can be claimed when restoration would take years of disruption. Test the actual cost, time, and expertise required. Some dependencies can only be managed, not cleanly undone; acknowledge that before entering them.

The pursuit of perfect independence can also prevent useful cooperation. Preserve enough capacity to negotiate and recover, while accepting dependencies that are transparent, bounded, and fairly governed.

## A first move

Choose one essential system and ask what would happen if its provider changed terms or withdrew tomorrow. Conduct a realistic exit review. Address the single dependency that would most seriously undermine the institution's ability to fulfill its duties.

**Connected patterns:** Leave Room for the Future (1), The Right to Reconsider (7), The Right to Exit and Belong (49), Delegated Intelligence (64), A Civilization That Can Reboot (79).

# 73 Diverse Systems

Two backup systems appear to provide redundancy. Both depend on the same supplier, the same network, and the same assumption about the weather. When that shared dependency fails, the second system offers little help.

Resilience depends on meaningful differences, not merely on having more copies.

**Therefore, maintain diversity in the ways essential needs are met. Examine shared dependencies and preserve alternatives that fail differently, while coordinating enough to keep the whole system usable.**

## A workable form

Map the sources, methods, institutions, and skills behind an essential service. Look for common points of failure hidden beneath different brand names or organizational charts. Include knowledge and financing as well as equipment.

Imagine a region supplying staple foods through a mixture of local production, trade relationships, storage, and different growing conditions. No component guarantees security. Their value lies partly in how their strengths and weaknesses complement one another.

Apply the principle to governance and knowledge too. Independent research teams, varied institutional forms, and different methods of inquiry can reduce dependence on one mistaken model. They need channels for comparison and cooperation without being forced into complete uniformity.

Maintain useful alternatives even when they appear inefficient under ordinary conditions. Explain the resilience they provide and test whether that benefit is real. A backup that nobody can operate is only an accounting entry.

## Where it fails

Diversity can become fragmentation. Incompatible systems may prevent cooperation during an emergency or impose needless costs on users. Preserve common interfaces and shared standards where they improve coordination without eliminating different approaches.

It can also protect inferior or harmful arrangements merely because they are different. Evaluate contribution to resilience alongside quality, rights, cost, and environmental effects. Variety is a means, not an exemption from judgment.

## A first move

Choose one essential service with an apparent backup. Trace both arrangements to their suppliers, power, networks, personnel, and assumptions. Address the most consequential dependency they share, then test whether the alternative can operate when that dependency is unavailable.

**Connected patterns:** Clean Energy in Many Hands (30), Enough Local Capacity (36), Commons at Every Scale (50), Failure That Stays Local (74), Reserves Before Scarcity (76).

# 74 Failure That Stays Local

A small fault becomes a regional crisis because every connected system passes the disturbance onward. Connections that create efficiency during ordinary operation can transmit damage during disruption.

A civilization needs boundaries that can contain failure without abandoning the people inside them.

**Therefore, organize essential systems into units that can limit the spread of disruption, continue critical functions where feasible, and reconnect safely. Design mutual aid alongside isolation.**

## A workable form

Identify how faults travel: through physical networks, software dependencies, supply chains, financial obligations, or shared decisions. Determine where separation can reduce propagation and what services must remain available during it.

Imagine a group of public facilities with locally supportable essential functions and agreed procedures for operating during a wider outage. The arrangement includes communication, qualified staff, resource limits, and assistance for people whose needs exceed local capacity.

Use controlled interfaces. Connections should have clear responsibilities, observable conditions, and procedures for interruption and restoration. A system should not automatically resume full operation merely because a signal appears normal again.

Consider the human geography of failure. A technically neat boundary may isolate a hospital from its suppliers or separate vulnerable residents from support. Design containment around the continuity of necessary care as well as the behavior of equipment.

## Where it fails

Local containment can become local abandonment. Wealthier units may protect themselves while poorer ones absorb disruption. Establish shared obligations, pooled support, and fair priorities before scarcity tests them.

Too many barriers can also make everyday service brittle or inefficient. Examine realistic failure paths and place boundaries where they materially reduce risk. Coordination and separation should be designed together.

## A first move

Map how one plausible failure could spread across three connected services. Identify a boundary that could interrupt the cascade. Test the interruption and reconnection procedures, including the assistance needed by people temporarily served through a limited local arrangement.

**Connected patterns:** Decisions at the Right Scale (8), Enough Local Capacity (36), Capability With Containment (66), Diverse Systems (73), Recovery Before Disaster (78).

# 75 Public Health for All Species

The clinic, the farm, the forest, and the water system are often governed separately. Their conditions nevertheless interact. A response confined to the place where illness becomes visible can miss the wider circumstances that sustain the problem.

Health protection benefits from looking across these boundaries while maintaining clear responsibilities.

**Therefore, coordinate the protection of human, animal, and environmental health. Build shared observation and response capacity while addressing the conditions that expose people and other beings to preventable harm.**

## A workable form

The WHO, FAO, UNEP, and WOAH One Health Joint Plan of Action for 2022–2026 connects the health of humans, animals, plants, and the environment. This pattern applies that integrative perspective to local institutional design, without supplying clinical or veterinary protocols. [S14]

Imagine a region where health workers, veterinarians, environmental specialists, and community representatives maintain regular working relationships. They know how to share relevant observations, protect sensitive information, and coordinate qualified investigation when concerns cross their usual boundaries.

Invest in ordinary capacity: accessible care, trusted communication, safe working conditions, environmental stewardship, and adequate staffing. Emergency response cannot fully compensate for systems weakened through years of neglect.

Include people closest to changing conditions. Farmers, animal carers, sanitation workers, and residents may observe problems before they appear in formal reporting. Make participation useful and safe, without placing the burden of diagnosis or response on untrained individuals.

## Where it fails

Integration can become a meeting without authority or resources. Define who acts, how decisions are made, and which institution remains responsible for each function.

Health protection can also justify disproportionate surveillance or indiscriminate harm to animals and livelihoods. Use appropriate expertise, assess alternatives, and make restrictions necessary, accountable, and revisable. Protecting one population should not make the suffering of another invisible.

## A first move

Bring the responsible human-health, animal-health, and environmental teams together around one shared local concern. Identify a gap in communication or responsibility and repair it through an agreed procedure, with privacy safeguards and a clear route to qualified assessment.

**Connected patterns:** Room for Wildness (20), Animals With Interests (22), End Pollution at the Source (26), Treaties for Shared Hazards (51), Practice the Emergency (77).

# 76 Reserves Before Scarcity

The spare capacity looked wasteful until the delivery failed. The stored supplies looked excessive until several communities needed them at once. Systems optimized only for ordinary conditions can become fragile at precisely the moment their services matter most.

A reserve buys time to respond without immediately forcing impossible choices.

**Therefore, maintain proportionate reserves of essential goods, capacity, money, and trained attention. Govern their use transparently and replenish them as part of ordinary operations.**

## A workable form

Identify essential functions and plausible interruptions. Estimate the time needed to restore supply or arrange alternatives, including uncertainty. The reserve should respond to those conditions rather than to a symbolic target detached from use.

Imagine a community service preparing for a prolonged disruption. It maintains suitable supplies, backup arrangements, staff coverage, and accessible information. Items that expire or deteriorate are rotated through normal use, and responsibility for checking the reserve is funded.

Agree on allocation before scarcity arrives. Prioritize essential needs and people facing the greatest vulnerability, with an intelligible process for exceptions and review. A reserve is a public protection only if people can reach it when needed.

Include social reserves: relationships that can mobilize help, staff who are not already exhausted, and budgets with some capacity to absorb surprise. A warehouse cannot substitute for every kind of readiness.

## Where it fails

Accumulation can become hoarding that worsens scarcity elsewhere. Coordinate across communities and consider how procurement affects current access for others.

Reserves can also hide poor planning or become a source of waste and corruption. Record condition, location, use, and replacement. Periodic exercises should test whether the reserve serves its purpose rather than merely whether it exists on paper.

## A first move

Choose one essential service and ask how long it could operate during a realistic interruption. Identify the limiting resource, then create or improve a proportionate reserve with a named caretaker, a rotation plan, and fair rules for release.

**Connected patterns:** Pay for Maintenance First (32), Enough Local Capacity (36), Diverse Systems (73), Practice the Emergency (77), Recovery Before Disaster (78).

# 77 Practice the Emergency

The plan assumes that someone will open the building, someone will contact isolated residents, and someone will know where the equipment is kept. In an exercise, it becomes clear that all three tasks belong to the same unavailable person.

Practice turns imagined readiness into observable capability.

**Therefore, rehearse plausible disruptions with the people who would actually respond and those who would need assistance. Use exercises to discover gaps, improve coordination, and revise plans without punishing honest learning.**

## A workable form

Begin with a bounded scenario grounded in local conditions. Include uncertainty, absent staff, damaged communications, and the needs of people poorly served by standard assumptions. Avoid making the exercise so elaborate that its central lessons disappear.

Imagine a neighborhood and its service providers rehearsing a severe outage. Participants test how information reaches people without reliable internet, how assistance is requested, and how responsibilities transfer when a key person is absent. Qualified authorities guide activities that require technical expertise.

Observe decisions as well as speed. Did participants understand priorities? Could someone challenge an unsafe assumption? Were the people needing help treated with dignity and given useful choices?

Finish with assigned improvements and a date to check them. Record what failed, why it mattered, and who will address it. The exercise is incomplete until learning changes resources, procedures, or relationships.

## Where it fails

Rehearsal can become a performance designed to reassure observers. Announced perfection reveals little about uncertain conditions. Use realistic complications and independent observation without humiliating participants.

Exercises can also cause distress, confusion, or unnecessary danger. Give appropriate notice, define boundaries, provide accessible participation, and allow people to step out. Preparedness should build trust rather than consume it.

## A first move

Run a short tabletop exercise for one plausible interruption. Ask each participant what they would do first and what they would need from others. Choose the three most consequential gaps, assign responsibility, and schedule a repeat after they have been addressed.

**Connected patterns:** Emergency Powers That End (53), Failure That Stays Local (74), Public Health for All Species (75), Reserves Before Scarcity (76), Remember the Near Misses (81).

# 78 Recovery Before Disaster

After the immediate danger passes, a second crisis begins: lost documents, uncertain housing, interrupted livelihoods, damaged institutions, and decisions made under pressure. Recovery can reproduce the vulnerabilities that made the disaster so harmful.

Planning for recovery beforehand gives people more choices when they are least able to invent them.

**Therefore, establish the relationships, resources, records, and fair procedures needed to restore life after disruption. Design recovery to reduce vulnerability and preserve affected people's influence over what comes next.**

## A workable form

Map what recovery would require beyond physical reconstruction: secure temporary housing, continuity of care and education, income support, access to records, ecological restoration, and the rebuilding of trust.

Imagine a coastal community preparing for severe damage to part of its settlement. Residents discuss restoration, adaptation, and possible relocation before an emergency. Plans identify support, decision rights, and protections against opportunistic displacement, while remaining revisable as circumstances change.

Preserve records in accessible, distributed forms, with appropriate privacy. Establish agreements for mutual assistance and identify how funds can be used accountably under difficult conditions. Recovery benefits from both speed and the ability to challenge unfair decisions.

Recognize that people recover at different rates. A reopened road or balanced budget does not establish that households, ecosystems, and social relationships have recovered. Use measures that follow those experiences over time.

## Where it fails

Rebuilding can become a rush to restore the same exposure to harm. Examine why the loss occurred and whether a different arrangement would better serve the community.

Improvement can also become a pretext for dispossession. People affected by the disruption should have a substantial voice in decisions about their homes and livelihoods. Emergency conditions do not erase their claims.

## A first move

Choose one plausible local disaster and describe the first year after the immediate response. Identify the decision most likely to be made unfairly under pressure. Establish a participatory procedure and practical support for making that decision before the pressure arrives.

**Connected patterns:** Housing That Can Change (29), A Fair Transition (45), Emergency Powers That End (53), Failure That Stays Local (74), Grief With Somewhere to Go (98).

# 79 A Civilization That Can Reboot

Complex systems depend on layers of other systems. Knowledge may be stored in a format that requires electricity, software, networks, credentials, and specialized equipment before a person can read the first useful sentence.

Continuity requires some paths that remain usable when several layers are missing.

**Therefore, preserve the knowledge, tools, skills, and cooperative institutions needed to restore essential capabilities after severe disruption. Design recovery paths for reduced conditions rather than assuming the full system remains available.**

## A workable form

Identify a practical sequence of dependencies. Restoring one service may require another to function first. Map those relationships and preserve ways to begin with the resources and expertise likely to remain.

Imagine a region maintaining a continuity collection for essential public services. It includes intelligible records, appropriate spare equipment, qualified training, and agreements among institutions. Selected materials remain accessible without the normal digital environment, and people periodically practice using them.

Preserve the social knowledge too: how to allocate fairly, resolve disputes, protect vulnerable people, and maintain legitimate authority. Technical reconstruction without these capacities can restore machinery while deepening domination.

Design the collection around actual stewardship. Someone must inspect materials, update obsolete guidance, teach skills, and test access. Multiple communities should hold complementary capacities so recovery does not depend on one supposedly invulnerable repository.

## Where it fails

Reboot thinking can become a fantasy of a small group inheriting the world after everyone else fails. Keep the purpose collective: reducing suffering, preserving rights, and helping ordinary communities recover.

Archives can also preserve dangerous or outdated instructions without context. Use qualified curation and explain limits, prerequisites, and the need for current professional judgment in high-stakes work.

## A first move

Choose one essential service and remove a major digital dependency from the imagined recovery conditions. Ask qualified staff whether they could still find, understand, and use the information needed to begin restoration. Repair the first dependency that makes recovery impossible.

**Connected patterns:** Keep the Capacity to Repair (9), Enough Local Capacity (36), Libraries That Survive (59), Human Skills Worth Keeping (68), No Irreversible Surrender (72).

# 80 Institutions With Successors

The founder retires. The grant ends. The administrator who remembers every exception leaves without a replacement. An institution can appear stable while depending on one person's memory, energy, or authority.

Longevity requires a credible transfer of responsibility, including the possibility of ending well.

**Therefore, design institutions to hand over their duties, resources, knowledge, and accountability. Prepare successors before they are urgently needed, and provide a responsible path to closure when the institution no longer serves its purpose.**

## A workable form

Identify functions that currently depend on a single person or relationship. Document enough to make them intelligible and provide supervised experience for others. Succession is learned through participation, not achieved by depositing a manual on someone's final day.

Imagine a community trust managing housing and land. It develops future governors, maintains clear records, reviews conflicts of interest, and explains its obligations to residents. Leadership changes through a legitimate process that preserves both continuity and room for improvement.

Transfer purposes as well as procedures. New stewards should understand why obligations exist, which compromises shaped them, and whose interests might otherwise disappear. They should also be able to question arrangements that have ceased to work.

Prepare for merger, replacement, or closure. Specify what happens to assets, records, staff, beneficiaries, and unfinished duties. An institution should not preserve itself by claiming that its responsibilities make its current form immortal.

## Where it fails

Succession can become hereditary control or the reproduction of a narrow circle. Create accessible routes for new participants and use criteria connected to actual duties.

Documentation can also conceal the absence of capability. Test whether successors can make decisions, access resources, and handle difficult cases. A named replacement without authority or preparation is not a succession plan.

## A first move

Ask which person's unexpected departure would most seriously impair your institution. Transfer one essential responsibility through supported practice, clarify the authority that accompanies it, and record what the exercise reveals about the institution's dependence.

**Connected patterns:** Guardians With Duties (27), Pay for Maintenance First (32), Ownership With Obligations (42), Finance That Can Wait (44), Libraries That Survive (59).

# 81 Remember the Near Misses

Nothing terrible happened. That is precisely why the warning may disappear. The door was almost left unlocked, the supply almost exhausted, the incorrect decision almost acted upon. Luck can be mistaken for evidence that the arrangement is sound.

A near miss is an opportunity to learn while the full cost of failure remains unpaid.

**Therefore, make it easy to report and investigate serious near misses. Identify the conditions that allowed them, share useful lessons, and verify that corrective action changes the system.**

## A workable form

Define which incidents deserve attention according to plausible consequences, not merely visible damage. Provide a reporting route that does not require people to predict every technical implication before raising a concern.

Imagine a service discovering that an essential task went unassigned during a staff transition. Another worker noticed in time. Review the assignment process, workload, information flow, and assumptions that created the gap, rather than treating the fortunate intervention as proof that informal vigilance is enough.

Distinguish ordinary error, system weakness, reckless conduct, and intentional wrongdoing through a fair process. People are more likely to report mistakes when investigation aims to understand them, but accountability should not disappear where conduct deserves it.

Share lessons at the appropriate scale while protecting privacy. A local event may reveal a vulnerability elsewhere. Record enough context to help others judge whether the lesson applies to their circumstances.

## Where it fails

Reporting can become an administrative burden with no visible response. Close the loop: tell participants what was learned, what changed, and what remains unresolved.

Organizations can also count reports as failures and reward silence. A rise in reporting may reflect greater trust rather than worsening performance. Examine severity, patterns, and corrective action instead of drawing conclusions from the count alone.

## A first move

Invite one team to describe a recent occasion when harm was narrowly avoided. Identify the safeguard that worked and the weakness it compensated for. Strengthen the system so the same outcome does not depend on the same person's luck or vigilance.

**Connected patterns:** Keep Dissent Safe (57), Measure What Matters (63), Practice the Emergency (77), Institutions With Successors (80), Publish the Failures (105).


# Part X  Neighbors in the cosmos

*Extend responsibility beyond Earth while taking uncertainty and distance seriously.*

# 82 Earth Is Not Disposable

Imagine a child watching the lights of an orbital settlement cross the evening sky. Expansion has made the universe feel more accessible. Below the child, the soil, water, atmosphere, and innumerable relationships of Earth still sustain the life doing the imagining.

A future beyond Earth should deepen responsibility for our first world rather than offer a story in which abandoning it becomes acceptable.

**Therefore, pursue exploration and possible settlement beyond Earth while treating the continuity of Earth's living systems as a central obligation. Evaluate new capabilities by how they affect that obligation and the people who remain here.**

## A workable form

Assess space projects alongside their terrestrial requirements and consequences: energy, materials, labor, environmental effects, public support, and competing needs. Benefits such as knowledge, observation, and useful technology deserve consideration without being assumed to justify every project.

Imagine a future settlement program whose charter includes responsibilities to Earth. It funds stewardship of its own material footprint, shares appropriate knowledge, and avoids presenting migration as a substitute for addressing damage. Participation in exploration does not erase obligations arising from activities at home.

Keep access and distribution visible. If only a few can leave a deteriorating place, the promise of escape may weaken their incentive to protect everyone else. Institutions should connect the benefits of expansion to continuing common responsibilities.

Preserve the emotional relationship as well. Education and culture can hold exploration together with attention to ordinary life: a shoreline, a migrating bird, a garden, a neighborhood. Wonder at distant worlds need not diminish wonder at this one.

## Where it fails

Earth-first language can become a blanket rejection of inquiry or a demand that no exploration occur until every other problem is solved. Evaluate specific projects and opportunity costs rather than imposing an impossible condition of universal completion.

The opposite failure is to treat hypothetical future abundance as permission for present damage. Promised substitutes should not be counted as established capabilities.

## A first move

For one proposed expansion or exploration project, write an account of its Earthbound obligations and plausible benefits. Identify which promises depend on uncertain future capabilities. Judge the present commitment using what can responsibly be supported now.

**Connected patterns:** Watersheds as Commons (19), Room for Wildness (20), Clean Energy in Many Hands (30), Account for the Full Cost (38), Space as a Shared Responsibility (83).

# 83 Space as a Shared Responsibility

A useful orbit, a scientific site, or a route of access can be degraded by actions whose benefits accrue to one actor while the costs spread widely. Distance from everyday life does not make these consequences ownerless.

As activity expands beyond Earth, shared responsibility must expand with it.

**Therefore, govern consequential space activity through cooperative arrangements that protect shared access, scientific value, environmental conditions, and future options. Make operators responsible for the continuing effects of their activities.**

## A workable form

Map who uses a region, who may use it later, and which actions could impair those possibilities. Include observation, communication, exploration, cultural interests, and the claims of places or life that may warrant protection independently of human use.

Imagine a future agreement governing a heavily used orbital region. Participants share relevant operational information, finance monitoring, establish duties for the full life of equipment, and provide fair procedures for disputes. The arrangement considers participants with limited resources rather than allowing early entrants to define access permanently.

Match coordination to consequences. Some decisions can remain with individual operators; others affect many parties and require shared standards or approval. Explain the basis for that distinction and review it as activity changes.

Avoid treating first arrival as a complete moral argument for control. Investment and effort can justify appropriate returns without automatically granting unlimited authority over a place or resource of wider significance.

## Where it fails

Shared governance can become domination by established powers under a universal label. Give less powerful communities meaningful participation and preserve transparency about benefits and burdens.

Broad agreements can also remain too vague to guide conduct. Assign specific duties, establish verification, and fund the institutions expected to enforce them. A shared responsibility needs identifiable responsible actors.

## A first move

Choose one space activity with effects beyond its operator. Identify the shared condition it depends on and the obligation needed to preserve that condition. Compare existing arrangements with that obligation and propose a concrete, reviewable improvement.

**Connected patterns:** Leave Room for the Future (1), Account for the Full Cost (38), Commons at Every Scale (50), Treaties for Shared Hazards (51), Worlds With Their Own Claims (84).

# 84 Worlds With Their Own Claims

A landscape without people is not necessarily a landscape without value. It may hold scientific evidence, geological uniqueness, the possibility of life, or forms of significance we have not learned to recognize.

The absence of an owner does not settle what visitors may do.

**Therefore, assess unfamiliar worlds and environments before altering them irreversibly. Preserve representative and exceptional places, protect possible life, and require reasons proportionate to the permanence of the change.**

## A workable form

Distinguish exploration, sampling, extraction, settlement, and large-scale transformation. Each creates different consequences and requires different evidence. A permission to investigate should not quietly become permission to remake the place.

NASA's planetary-protection work addresses avoiding harmful biological contamination between Earth and other solar-system environments. This pattern draws on that concern while making a broader ethical proposal: places may deserve protection for more than their immediate usefulness to us. [S11]

Imagine a future mission finding ambiguous evidence in a previously unexamined environment. It preserves the evidence, seeks independent assessment, and postpones activities that could destroy the possibility of learning more. Uncertainty becomes a reason to investigate carefully, not an automatic verdict of either prohibition or permission.

Create protected areas and staged decisions where appropriate. Record what has been altered and why. Preserve options for future investigators whose methods and ethical understanding may exceed our own.

## Where it fails

Absolute preservation can make any encounter impossible, because observation itself can have effects. Compare methods and pursue inquiry that is proportionate, informative, and limited in its irreversible consequences.

Speculation about possible value can also be applied selectively to block others while exempting powerful actors. Use transparent criteria and independent review across comparable activities.

## A first move

For one proposed intervention in a little-understood environment, identify what could be permanently lost before its significance is known. Design an earlier stage that gathers useful evidence while leaving that feature intact.

**Connected patterns:** Leave Room for the Future (1), Proportionate Caution (6), Room for Wildness (20), Space as a Shared Responsibility (83), Contact Without Contamination (89).

# 85 Settlements That Can Refuse

Imagine living in a settlement where the same organization controls air, water, employment, housing, communication, and the only transport home. A contract signed before departure cannot by itself make every later demand freely accepted.

Extreme dependence can turn an ambitious settlement into a form of captivity.

**Therefore, establish enforceable rights, independent representation, and practical routes to refuse, challenge, and leave within highly dependent settlements. Separate essential life support from obedience to employers or political leaders.**

## A workable form

Before settlement, define governance, service obligations, dispute resolution, emergency authority, and responsibilities for return or relocation. Explain which promises depend on uncertain capabilities. People cannot meaningfully consent to risks hidden behind promotional confidence.

Imagine a remote habitat with resident representation, protected communication, independent review, and essential services that cannot be withdrawn as an ordinary disciplinary measure. Employment disputes do not automatically threaten a person's access to the conditions of survival.

Build redundancy in authority as well as equipment. Independent advocates, external accountability, and transparent records can provide recourse when physical exit is temporarily impossible. These institutions need resources and access sufficient to act.

Consider children and later arrivals whose lives were not chosen by the original settlers. A founding bargain cannot determine every future resident's rights. Governance must adapt as a temporary mission becomes a community.

## Where it fails

A nominal right to leave is empty when transport is unavailable or unaffordable. Be explicit about constraints and provide the strongest feasible protections while working to reduce them.

Collective safety can also require limits on individual action in tightly coupled environments. Such limits should be necessary, proportionate, reviewable, and administered through legitimate rules. Dependence does not justify unlimited command.

## A first move

For any highly dependent residential project, trace the consequences of a resident saying no to an employer or leader. Identify where refusal threatens essential services or all practical recourse. Redesign that relationship before accepting residents.

**Connected patterns:** A Floor Beneath Everyone (4), Consent as a Daily Practice (13), Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove (48), The Right to Exit and Belong (49), Emergency Powers That End (53).

# 86 Listen Before Sending

A message cannot always be recalled. Its meaning may exceed the sender's intention, and the decision to transmit may affect people who never participated in it.

In a hypothetical encounter with extraterrestrial intelligence, the ability to communicate would not by itself establish the authority to initiate consequential communication.

**Therefore, develop observation, understanding, and shared deliberation before undertaking intentional communication with potentially civilization-wide consequences. Treat uncertainty honestly and distinguish the decision to investigate from the decision to address another intelligence.**

## A workable form

Ask what is known about the recipient, the medium, the possible reach, and the irreversibility of the act. Separate demonstrated consequences from conjectures. Avoid confident claims that a particular message would be either harmless or catastrophic when the evidence cannot support them.

Imagine a future research group proposing a response to a credible signal. Scientific assessment examines what has actually been detected. A broader process considers whether to respond, who should participate, and what authority could justify the choice.

The IAA's June 2026 SETI declaration calls for broadly representative international consultation before a reply to a confirmed detection, with no reply pending that process. This is professional guidance rather than a complete, binding global decision system. [S12]

Prepare deliberative capacity before the pressure of a dramatic discovery. Clarify responsibilities, public communication, and the treatment of disagreement. Readiness includes the ability to wait while important questions remain unresolved.

## Where it fails

Caution can become an indefinite veto held by whoever controls the agenda. Define what evidence and deliberation would permit a decision, while acknowledging that some uncertainty may remain.

Enthusiasm can also treat technical access as a mandate to act for everyone. Require reasons that address those who bear the consequences without controlling the transmitter.

## A first move

Draft a decision process for a hypothetical proposal to send a consequential interstellar message. Separate scientific verification, public deliberation, authorization, and execution. Identify who can pause the process and what would justify resuming it.

**Connected patterns:** Leave Room for the Future (1), Proportionate Caution (6), Treaties for Shared Hazards (51), Confirm Before Announcing (87), No One Speaks for Earth (88).

# 87 Confirm Before Announcing

A surprising observation can become a global story before anyone understands it. Once a dramatic interpretation takes hold, corrections must compete with the emotional force of the first announcement.

The public deserves both timely information and an honest account of what has actually been established.

**Therefore, distinguish a candidate observation from a verified interpretation. Seek independent examination, preserve the evidence, and communicate uncertainty clearly before presenting extraordinary conclusions as fact.**

## A workable form

Establish procedures before a candidate discovery. Specify how evidence is recorded, who can examine it, which alternative explanations must be considered, and how public inquiries will be handled. Protect researchers from pressure to provide certainty prematurely.

Imagine several observatories examining an unusual signal. They compare independent observations, instruments, methods, and possible sources of error. The account of the work explains which conclusions are supported and which remain open, including the possibility that no definitive interpretation will emerge.

The June 2026 SETI declaration emphasizes authentication, independent examination where possible, careful handling of candidate evidence, and open reporting of a confirmed finding. Its guidance also calls for correcting previously publicized candidates that prove non-extraterrestrial. [S12]

Preserve data and analysis in accessible, resilient repositories. A claim of major significance should remain open to later scrutiny. Public communication needs responsible institutions, not a single exhausted researcher expected to answer every demand.

## Where it fails

Verification can become a pretext for secrecy or control over credit. Explain the process and its limits, and provide accurate updates when needed to address public confusion without overstating the result.

Speed can also substitute for rigor. A prominent announcement cannot make weak evidence stronger. Institutions should reward careful correction and acknowledge uncertainty without treating either as embarrassment.

## A first move

Create a communication template that separates observation, analysis, confidence, alternative explanations, and next steps. Test it on a hypothetical extraordinary finding with both technical reviewers and non-specialist readers. Revise any wording that turns possibility into apparent confirmation.

**Connected patterns:** Shared Reality (55), Show the Work (56), Keep Dissent Safe (57), Libraries That Survive (59), Listen Before Sending (86).

# 88 No One Speaks for Earth

A discoverer may be first to receive a signal. A government may possess a powerful transmitter. A company may own the relevant equipment. None of these facts alone makes that actor the legitimate representative of a planet.

Encountering another intelligence would intensify an old political question: who can speak for whom, and under what authority?

**Therefore, distinguish discovery and technical capability from representative legitimacy. Build plural, accountable processes for decisions made in Earth's name, and describe the limits of any mandate honestly.**

## A workable form

Separate factual communication from collective commitment. Reporting an observation is different from promising cooperation, disclosing sensitive information, or asserting values on behalf of all inhabitants. Each act needs an appropriate basis of authority.

Imagine a future international contact forum. It includes scientific expertise, diverse political and cultural perspectives, affected communities, and advocates for interests that cannot directly participate. Its decisions and unresolved disagreements are visible rather than packaged as a fictitious planetary consensus.

The 2026 SETI declaration points toward consultation through the United Nations and other broadly representative bodies before replying to a confirmed detection. The legitimacy and design of such consultation would still require substantial work. [S12]

Avoid assuming the other party is unified. An unfamiliar civilization may contain disagreements, hierarchies, or multiple forms of agency. Interpreting one sender as the voice of an entire world could repeat the same error in reverse.

## Where it fails

Demanding perfect representation can make any decision impossible. Seek a defensible process with explicit limits, review, and accountability rather than an impossible claim to unanimous authority.

A representative forum can also become a stage on which powerful actors dominate. Examine agenda setting, access to evidence, translation, resources, and the real ability to dissent.

## A first move

Write two versions of a hypothetical contact statement: one describing what a specific institution knows, and another claiming to commit Earth to an action. Identify the additional authority the second would require and design a process that could credibly examine that claim.

**Connected patterns:** A Seat for the Absent (3), Citizens Who Deliberate (47), Treaties for Shared Hazards (51), Personhood Beyond Resemblance (71), Listen Before Sending (86).

# 89 Contact Without Contamination

Encounter can change what it seeks to understand. A sample may alter the environment from which it is taken. A visitor may carry something invisible. An interpretation may impose familiar categories on a form of life that does not fit them.

Careful contact preserves the possibility of learning without imposing avoidable irreversible harm.

**Therefore, approach unfamiliar environments and possible life through staged, independently reviewed procedures that limit contamination in both directions. Extend the same discipline to information and interpretation without inventing unsupported threats.**

## A workable form

NASA distinguishes forward contamination of other environments by Earth material from backward contamination associated with material returned to Earth. Actual mission requirements depend on the destination, activity, and scientific assessment. [S11]

Imagine a future mission encountering evidence that might indicate life. Qualified teams protect the site and samples, review the evidence independently, and limit further intervention while deciding what can responsibly be learned next. The procedures follow current expertise rather than a generic rule from a philosophical book.

If contact involved intelligence, additional questions would arise about communication, consent, and the effects of exchange. Treat these as questions to investigate. Do not assume that biological resemblance, technical sophistication, or a seemingly friendly message settles them.

Keep information practices proportionate. Unfamiliar data need careful preservation, provenance, and appropriate technical handling. Speculative risks should be distinguished from demonstrated hazards, with restrictions explained and reviewed.

## Where it fails

Protection can become an excuse for monopolizing discovery or withholding information indefinitely. Use independent oversight, bounded restrictions, and public reasoning wherever possible.

Openness can also become an automatic demand to circulate everything immediately. Where a specific, credible risk exists, staged access may preserve both inquiry and safety. The burden is to justify the restriction and revisit it.

## A first move

For a hypothetical encounter, distinguish physical contamination, loss of evidence, interpretive error, and information risk. Assign each question to appropriate expertise and identify what can be investigated without making the consequences irreversible.

**Connected patterns:** Proportionate Caution (6), Show the Work (56), Capability With Containment (66), Worlds With Their Own Claims (84), Confirm Before Announcing (87).

# 90 The Right to Remain Unknown

Curiosity can feel innocent to the curious and invasive to its subject. The ability to locate, observe, or address another community does not necessarily create an entitlement to its attention, information, or participation.

Respectful encounter includes the possibility that contact is unwanted.

**Therefore, recognize a presumptive claim to privacy, distance, and nonparticipation where beings or communities have relevant interests. Investigate carefully, seek consent where possible, and require strong reasons for overriding a clear refusal.**

## A workable form

Begin with familiar relationships. People and communities may have good reasons to limit exposure: safety, cultural continuity, freedom from exploitation, or simply a desire to live without an audience. Curiosity alone rarely resolves those claims.

Imagine a future encounter in which an unfamiliar intelligence communicates a wish for distance. Before treating refusal as hostility or a puzzle to overcome, examine whether the message is understood, who it represents, and what forms of noninterference are feasible.

Distinguish nonparticipation from permission to harm others. A claim to privacy does not automatically justify imposing serious external damage. Where interests conflict, seek the least intrusive means of addressing the concrete problem.

Allow for uncertainty about agency and boundaries. An apparently silent environment may not contain a community capable of consent; an apparently unified community may include individuals who want contact. Avoid inventing certainty to make the encounter easier to govern.

## Where it fails

Respect for isolation can protect an oppressive leadership that prevents members from seeking help. Attend to individual rights and credible evidence of coercion without assuming every unfamiliar social arrangement authorizes intervention.

The pursuit of knowledge can also become a self-justifying demand for access. Ask what purpose the inquiry serves, what harm it may impose, and whether less intrusive means are available.

## A first move

Review one research, media, or exploration project that depends on access to others. Identify how a subject can decline or set limits. Make that choice practical, document its consequences, and establish a fair process for exceptional conflicts.

**Connected patterns:** Consent as a Daily Practice (13), The Right to Exit and Belong (49), Local Knowledge With Consent (60), Worlds With Their Own Claims (84), Hospitality Across Difference (96).


# Part XI  Lives worth living

*Make room for joy, beauty, culture, spiritual inquiry, and experiences that need no economic justification.*

# 91 Time That Is Your Own

The afternoon contains no appointment. Someone walks without tracking the distance, reads without extracting a lesson, or sits beside a friend whose company needs no justification. Such time is easy to call unproductive and difficult to replace with anything else.

A civilization can become richer in goods while leaving its people poorer in the freedom to inhabit their lives.

**Therefore, protect a meaningful share of time from compulsory work, administrative burden, and relentless demands for attention. Make this freedom available across differences of income, care responsibility, and bodily capacity.**

## A workable form

Count the time required to maintain an ordinary life: paid work, commuting, care, household tasks, paperwork, and recovery from exhaustion. What remains may be too fragmented or uncertain to feel like freedom.

Imagine a neighborhood reducing unnecessary journeys through nearby services, reliable transport, shared facilities, and accessible care. These changes return time without requiring every resident to become more efficient. Workplace arrangements and income security determine whether that time can actually be kept.

Share the gains of useful automation through shorter or more flexible work, dependable services, and reduced administrative friction. Ask whether the tool frees its user or merely allows someone else to demand more.

Leave the use of free time open. Some people will make art, organize, learn, or explore. Others will rest. A right to time should not depend on presenting an improving activity to an approving authority.

## Where it fails

Leisure for one person can depend on another's invisible overwork. Examine who cleans, cooks, coordinates, and remains available. The distribution of time belongs in the account of fairness.

Freedom can also become isolation when public places, relationships, and opportunities are absent. Protect time together with the conditions that make it usable, while respecting solitude and the choice to do very little.

## A first move

Ask a group of residents or workers which recurring demand consumes time without adding value to their lives. Remove or simplify one such demand. Check who actually receives the time saved and whether another person has quietly inherited the work.

**Connected patterns:** A Common Table (14), Work With Meaning (17), Share the Gains of Invention (39), Pay for Care (43), Freedom From Machine Persuasion (67).

# 92 A Living Culture

The song survives because someone sings it differently. The recipe changes when a new ingredient arrives. A language acquires words for inventions its earlier speakers could not have imagined.

Culture endures through use, argument, affection, and invention. Preserving it as an untouchable object can separate it from the people whose lives give it meaning.

**Therefore, sustain the people, places, languages, and practices through which culture is made and transmitted. Allow communities to preserve, reinterpret, and change their inheritance without surrendering it to exploitation or compulsory conformity.**

## A workable form

Support spaces where people practice together: workshops, kitchens, rehearsal rooms, festivals, libraries, and informal meeting places. Pay cultural workers and make participation possible for those without spare income or abundant free time.

Imagine a community sustaining a language across generations. Children encounter it in ordinary activities, adults can learn without shame, and creators use it for new stories, humor, science, and daily communication. Recordings and archives help, but living use remains central.

Let transmission move in more than one direction. Elders may hold knowledge that younger people need; younger people may discover forms that make the inheritance newly usable. Disagreement can be part of continuity rather than proof that continuity has failed.

Respect the terms under which cultural material is shared. Attribution, consent, compensation, and context matter, particularly where communities have experienced extraction or suppression. Exchange should create relationships rather than simply move valuable material toward the strongest market.

## Where it fails

Authenticity can become a weapon against members whose lives depart from an approved image. A culture should not require every person to represent it in the same way.

Commercial success can also narrow culture to what outsiders recognize and buy. Preserve room for work that matters locally, changes slowly, or refuses easy translation.

## A first move

Choose one cultural practice that people want to continue. Ask practitioners what most threatens its living transmission: space, time, language, income, access, or confidence. Address that condition with them, rather than beginning with a promotional campaign about its importance.

**Connected patterns:** Learning Across a Lifetime (58), Libraries That Survive (59), Local Knowledge With Consent (60), Time That Is Your Own (91), Freedom to Be Strange (97).

# 93 Rituals for Change

A person becomes a parent, leaves a home, changes a name, joins a community, or ends a long chapter of work. The legal or administrative event may happen quickly. The emotional and social transition often needs more time and witnesses.

Ritual gives change a form that people can inhabit together.

**Therefore, create voluntary, adaptable ways to mark consequential transitions. Let people acknowledge what is ending, recognize what is beginning, and make the promises or support appropriate to the change.**

## A workable form

Begin with the needs of the participants rather than an inherited script. Some transitions call for celebration, some for mourning, some for repair, and some for a mixture that resists a single mood.

Imagine a neighborhood welcoming new residents. The occasion introduces people, explains shared responsibilities, offers practical help, and invites newcomers to contribute something of their own. It does not require a performance of gratitude or an oath to every local custom.

Create room for different beliefs and for no religious belief. A shared meal, a story, a walk, an object passed between people, or a moment of silence can carry meaning without everyone interpreting it identically.

Connect symbolic acts to continuing relationships. A ceremony promising support to a new parent should lead to actual help. A public apology should be linked to repair. Ritual can make responsibility visible; it cannot fulfill the responsibility by itself.

## Where it fails

Ritual can become compulsory conformity, especially when refusal carries social penalties. Provide dignified alternatives and allow people to shape how much they disclose.

It can also become a polished event that obscures unresolved conflict. Acknowledge disagreement where it exists. Not every transition ends in reconciliation, and not every participant is ready for the same kind of closure.

## A first move

Identify a transition your community currently handles only through paperwork or silence. Ask those experiencing it what acknowledgment would help. Create a modest, optional practice with one concrete commitment that continues after the occasion ends.

**Connected patterns:** Safe Beginnings (10), A Dignified Death (18), The Right to Exit and Belong (49), A Living Culture (92), Grief With Somewhere to Go (98).

# 94 Wonder Without Compulsion

A child asks why the moon follows the car. An adult hears a piece of music that makes the room unfamiliar. Someone looks through a microscope and discovers that an ordinary drop of water contains a world.

Wonder opens attention. It does not need to produce a useful invention or a shared doctrine to justify its place in a life.

**Therefore, make encounters with nature, art, inquiry, and mystery widely available. Protect the freedom to explore meaning without requiring a prescribed belief or a profitable result.**

## A workable form

Provide accessible places and occasions for discovery: libraries, gardens, observatories, workshops, performances, and quiet public spaces. Include people whose income, disability, location, or schedule would otherwise keep them out.

Imagine a town opening a public building one evening each month for music, experiments, storytelling, and looking at the sky. Participants can ask basic questions without embarrassment. Some return with expertise; others come simply to experience something unfamiliar.

Let different forms of wonder coexist. Scientific explanation can deepen astonishment; religious or philosophical traditions may offer other forms of meaning. Shared access need not require agreement about ultimate questions.

Preserve unprogrammed encounters. A tree, a view, a street musician, or a well-placed bench can invite attention without turning it into a scheduled activity. Not every valuable experience needs an institution around it.

## Where it fails

Wonder can be used to discourage questions about evidence or authority. A moving experience does not establish every claim made in its name. Protect curiosity in both directions: toward possibility and toward careful examination.

Institutions can also turn wonder into a status performance, making people feel they need the correct vocabulary to enter. Offer welcome without requiring expertise, and let participants respond in their own ways.

## A first move

Create one recurring, accessible opportunity to encounter something beyond ordinary routine. Remove a practical barrier to attendance, then leave room for participants to follow their own questions. Judge the occasion partly by whether people feel freer to notice and inquire afterward.

**Connected patterns:** A Place to Be Alone (15), Beauty in Ordinary Places (33), Learning Across a Lifetime (58), Uncertain Minds Deserve Care (70), Beauty Without Possession (99).

# 95 Games Worth Playing

For an hour, the square belongs to a game. People accept an invented constraint, argue about a rule, attempt something difficult, and laugh at a failure that would be frightening elsewhere. Play creates a temporary world in which effort need not be justified by survival.

A good game can offer challenge, companionship, and discovery while allowing people to return freely to the rest of life.

**Therefore, make room for voluntary play with fair rules, accessible entry, meaningful challenge, and the ability to stop. Protect games from incentives that exploit compulsion or turn every activity into ranking.**

## A workable form

Provide places, equipment, time, and social permission for play across ages and abilities. Include cooperative games, solitary puzzles, improvisation, physical activity, and forms that do not require exceptional skill.

Imagine a neighborhood play space where participants can adapt rules to include different bodies and experience levels. Serious competition has a place, but it does not occupy every hour or determine who belongs.

Give players influence over the conditions. Rules should be understandable, disputes manageable, and participation genuinely voluntary. In digital games, make costs and commercial incentives visible and avoid designs that depend on users losing control of their time or spending.

Keep some activities outside measurement. A game may have scores; a life need not become one. The pleasure of trying, imagining, and being together can matter independently of winning or producing a record.

## Where it fails

Play can reproduce humiliation and exclusion. Traditions of teasing or initiation deserve examination when newcomers consistently bear the cost. Challenge should remain compatible with dignity.

The language of play can also be used to intensify work through points, rankings, and rewards without increasing freedom. Ask whether participants can refuse the game and whether its rules serve purposes they actually endorse.

## A first move

Invite a mixed group to play something whose rules can be changed. Notice who participates, who withdraws, and why. Adjust one rule or condition so more people can experience meaningful challenge without requiring everyone to enjoy the same activity.

**Connected patterns:** A Village Around Every Child (11), Public Space Without a Purchase (34), Freedom From Machine Persuasion (67), Time That Is Your Own (91), Freedom to Be Strange (97).

# 96 Hospitality Across Difference

The visitor does not know where to sit, what food contains, which customs matter, or how to decline politely. The host may not know what the visitor needs. Hospitality begins by making this uncertainty easier to navigate.

Welcoming someone is a practical act of creating room without demanding that difference disappear.

**Therefore, establish ordinary practices that help strangers and newcomers participate with dignity. Make expectations understandable, offer meaningful choices, and allow mutual learning within fair boundaries.**

## A workable form

Attend to first encounters: signs, language, seating, food, accessibility, introductions, and the tone of the person answering a question. Small arrangements can determine whether a place feels available or guarded.

Imagine a community meal with clear information about ingredients, several ways to participate, and someone who notices people arriving alone. Newcomers can join without explaining their entire history or becoming representatives of a group.

Make hospitality reciprocal without making it transactional. Guests may contribute stories, skills, attention, or future help, but welcome should not depend on immediately proving their usefulness. Hosts can also learn to become guests.

Define boundaries that protect everyone. A shared space can welcome disagreement while refusing intimidation or abuse. Explain expectations consistently and provide a fair way to address conflict rather than relying on insiders' unspoken judgments.

## Where it fails

Hospitality can preserve hierarchy when the host alone decides whether others may ever belong. Create routes from visitor to participant and, where appropriate, to shared governance.

Welcoming language can also hide practical exclusion. If participation requires money, fluency, mobility, or free time that people do not have, the invitation remains incomplete.

## A first move

Ask someone unfamiliar with a community space to describe their first ten minutes there. Identify the point at which they felt unsure, unwelcome, or dependent on insider knowledge. Change that condition and test the welcome again with a different newcomer.

**Connected patterns:** A Common Table (14), Public Space Without a Purchase (34), The Right to Exit and Belong (49), The Right to Remain Unknown (90), A Living Culture (92).

# 97 Freedom to Be Strange

The unusual person is tolerated until a committee asks whether their life is useful, respectable, or representative. A society can speak warmly about diversity while making everyday existence difficult for anyone who departs too far from its preferred forms.

Freedom includes room for lives that other people do not understand or wish to imitate.

**Therefore, protect harmless eccentricity, unconventional relationships, minority expression, and experiments in living. Require a concrete account of harm before using collective power to enforce conformity.**

## A workable form

Distinguish discomfort from injury. An unfamiliar appearance, belief, art form, or household arrangement may unsettle observers without violating their rights. Public rules should be able to explain the legitimate purpose of a restriction beyond preserving familiarity.

Imagine a neighborhood where residents differ greatly in dress, schedules, celebrations, and ideas of a good life. Shared rules address noise, safety, access, and fair use of space. They leave substantial room for variation within those obligations.

Provide material conditions for difference. People need housing, work, care, and public access that do not depend on performing a single acceptable identity. A freedom available only to the independently wealthy is a thin freedom.

Keep the right to change. An unconventional identity should not become a role a person must maintain for an audience. People may become more ordinary, more unusual, or simply harder to categorize over time.

## Where it fails

Claims of individuality can excuse exploitation or disregard for shared responsibilities. Apply the same standards of consent, safety, and accountability to unusual and conventional arrangements alike.

Celebrating strangeness can also turn people into spectacles. Respect privacy and the choice not to explain. A person's value does not depend on providing inspiration, novelty, or entertainment to others.

## A first move

Review one rule that limits expression or participation. State the concrete harm it prevents and examine whether a less restrictive rule would address that harm. Remove requirements whose main function is to make everyone resemble the people already in charge.

**Connected patterns:** Many Ways to Flourish (5), Consent as a Daily Practice (13), Rights That Majorities Cannot Remove (48), The Right to Exit and Belong (49), A Living Culture (92).

# 98 Grief With Somewhere to Go

After the first week, the messages become less frequent. The person who has lost someone or something remains inside a changed world while ordinary schedules resume around them.

Grief needs places, relationships, and time in which it can be present without being treated as a problem that must quickly disappear.

**Therefore, make room for mourning in private and collective life. Offer practical support, voluntary forms of remembrance, and continuing companionship without prescribing a timetable or a single acceptable expression.**

## A workable form

Recognize different losses: a person, an animal companion, a home, a capacity, a community, or a living landscape. Their meanings differ, and people should not have to prove that their grief fits an approved hierarchy before receiving care.

Imagine a workplace whose support extends beyond a short absence. Colleagues offer specific help, managers allow reasonable flexibility, and the person can choose how much to disclose. Support remains available after the public moment of sympathy has passed.

Create places for memory that people can use in different ways: a garden, a gathering, an archive, a walk, or a quiet room. Some will want shared ritual; others will want solitude. Both can belong within a caring community.

Connect people with qualified help when they want or need it, while allowing ordinary companionship to remain ordinary. A friend need not become a therapist to bring food, listen, or return next month.

## Where it fails

Public mourning can become compulsory performance. People may grieve differently, feel ambivalent, or decline participation. Respect those differences without demanding an explanation.

Grief can also be mobilized to justify retaliation or permanent hostility. Acknowledge loss while preserving responsibility for what follows. Suffering deserves care; it does not make every response to suffering just.

## A first move

Choose one setting where loss is currently handled through silence or a brief formal gesture. Ask affected people what ongoing support would be welcome. Establish one dependable practice that remains available after attention has moved elsewhere.

**Connected patterns:** Care Across a Lifetime (16), A Dignified Death (18), Memory of Harm (62), Recovery Before Disaster (78), Rituals for Change (93).

# 99 Beauty Without Possession

The view is beautiful until a fence makes it available only to those who can pay. The object is beautiful until its removal leaves the place it came from diminished. Admiration can become a desire to control what should remain encountered on other terms.

Beauty can enrich a life without becoming part of a person's inventory.

**Therefore, create opportunities to encounter beauty through shared access, care, attention, and temporary participation. Protect the places, beings, and makers involved from being reduced to objects of possession.**

## A workable form

Support public landscapes, everyday architecture, performances, gardens, and collections that people can enjoy without purchasing ownership. Include ordinary routes and neighborhoods rather than concentrating beauty in a few celebrated destinations.

Imagine a town restoring access to a riverside while protecting habitat and the privacy of nearby residents. Paths, seating, seasonal restrictions, and maintenance allow encounter without assuming every part of the place must be continuously available to everyone.

Practice forms of temporary relationship: borrowing an object, attending a performance, visiting a place, learning a craft, or helping care for a garden. These can create attachment without requiring exclusive control.

Respect the labor and conditions behind what is admired. Artists, craftspeople, gardeners, and caretakers need support. Shared access does not require their work to be unpaid, nor does appreciation entitle an audience to a person's presence or private life.

## Where it fails

The language of common beauty can justify taking cultural objects or opening sensitive places without consent. Ask who has authority, who bears the burden of access, and what must remain protected.

Beauty can also become an official taste imposed on everyone. Encourage variety, local participation, and room for difficult or unfamiliar work. Public support should not require universal approval of every encounter.

## A first move

Identify one source of beauty people could enjoy more fairly without owning it. Work with its makers or caretakers to improve access while funding care and respecting limits. Notice whether the arrangement deepens attention or simply increases consumption.

**Connected patterns:** Room for Wildness (20), Beauty in Ordinary Places (33), Public Space Without a Purchase (34), Local Knowledge With Consent (60), Wonder Without Compulsion (94).


# Part XII  Putting the language to work

*Turn large commitments into local action and let experience improve the book.*

# 100 Begin With a Place

The book is large enough to make action feel small. A reader can spend years comparing civilizations while the leaking roof, inaccessible doorway, neglected stream, or lonely neighbor remains unchanged.

A place gives the language something real to answer to.

**Therefore, begin where you can observe consequences, form relationships, and accept responsibility. Learn the place before deciding which patterns it needs, and connect local action to wider obligations.**

## A workable form

Choose a place at a workable scale: a household, street, school, workplace, watershed, or shared institution. Define the question you hope to address without pretending that the boundary contains every relevant effect.

Imagine a community considering the future of an underused public building. Before announcing a project, spend time there and nearby. Learn who already uses it, who avoids it, which needs are unmet, and which informal relationships a redesign might disrupt.

Talk with people at different times and through different channels. A meeting in the evening reaches some residents and excludes others. Observation should include maintenance workers, neighbors, occasional users, and people whose absence may reveal a barrier.

Start an account of the place: its history, resources, constraints, conflicts, and sources of affection. Keep it open to correction. The purpose is to develop attention and relationships strong enough to support a useful decision.

## Where it fails

Local action can become an excuse to ignore structural conditions. A neighborhood may improve a service while the funding system continues to undermine it. Identify which problems require a wider coalition or a different scale of authority.

Attachment can also become territorial possession. The people most vocal about loving a place do not automatically speak for everyone who depends on it. Include newcomers and those whose connection is less visible.

## A first move

Spend time in one place without proposing a solution. Ask three people with different relationships to it what works, what hurts, and what they fear losing. Write a short account they can correct, then choose one question worth investigating together.

**Connected patterns:** Decisions at the Right Scale (8), Settlements That Belong to Place (28), Public Space Without a Purchase (34), Local Knowledge With Consent (60), Map Who Bears the Cost (101).

# 101 Map Who Bears the Cost

The proposal describes benefits for users and savings for the organization. Missing are the night worker, the downstream community, the animal habitat, and the person who will inherit the maintenance obligation.

Every project has a boundary around its account of consequences. Ethical attention begins by examining who falls outside it.

**Therefore, identify who benefits, who bears costs, who decides, and who cannot speak within the process. Follow consequences across places, species, institutions, and time before fixing the design.**

## A workable form

Begin with direct participants, then follow the project's dependencies and effects. Include workers, neighbors, suppliers, excluded users, caretakers, and future stewards. Consider animals and ecological systems through appropriate knowledge and accountable representation.

Return to the public building. A new use may benefit daytime visitors while increasing evening noise, unpaid coordination, or rents nearby. An apparently vacant room may already support an informal practice. Identify these relationships before describing the project as a universal improvement.

Distinguish severity, likelihood, reversibility, and distribution. A small inconvenience shared widely is different from a serious burden imposed on a few people with little recourse. Do not collapse every effect into a single score that conceals these differences.

Ask affected people to correct the map. Where direct participation is impossible, state who represents the interest, what evidence they use, and where uncertainty remains. Absence should create a duty of care rather than an empty space available for convenient assumptions.

## Where it fails

Mapping can become an endless exercise that postpones necessary action. Focus first on consequential effects and update the account as learning continues.

It can also become a record of burdens the project has no intention of addressing. Connect each major concern to a design change, protection, compensation where appropriate, or a clear reason the proposal should not proceed.

## A first move

For one live proposal, draw four columns: benefits, burdens, decision power, and missing voices. Put names or clearly defined groups into each. Find the largest mismatch between burden and influence, then change the process to address it.

**Connected patterns:** A Seat for the Absent (3), Animals With Interests (22), Guardians With Duties (27), Account for the Full Cost (38), Begin With a Place (100).

# 102 Assemble a Working Language

One pattern rarely resolves a real problem. A public space needs maintenance, access, beauty, safety, freedom, and rules for sharing it. Applying one principle without its neighbors can produce the very harm the broader language tries to prevent.

The work is to compose a set of relationships appropriate to the situation.

**Therefore, select a small group of connected patterns around a concrete purpose. Make their tensions explicit and develop a design that serves them together rather than treating them as independent boxes to check.**

## A workable form

State the purpose in ordinary language. For the public building, it might be: a dependable place where residents can meet, learn, rest, and organize without being required to buy something.

Choose patterns that illuminate the main conditions. Public Space Without a Purchase concerns access; Pay for Maintenance First concerns continuity; A Place to Be Alone protects quieter uses; Hospitality Across Difference shapes welcome; Power Must Explain Itself makes management answerable.

Examine tensions through concrete situations. An open evening gathering may disturb nearby homes. A quiet room may reduce space available for communal activities. Explore schedules, acoustic design, staffing, and fair rules rather than declaring that one value always wins.

Write a short account of the composition: which patterns matter, how the design responds, what compromises remain, and what evidence would prompt revision. Include the costs and responsibilities needed to make the arrangement real.

## Where it fails

The language can become a prestige vocabulary attached after decisions are made. Each selected pattern should change a question, feature, obligation, or test. If it changes nothing, its presence may be ornamental.

Trying to apply all 108 patterns to every project can overwhelm judgment. Begin with those that matter most, follow consequential connections, and remain open to omissions identified by others.

## A first move

Choose five patterns for a current project. Write one sentence explaining what each requires in this place, then identify the hardest conflict among them. Produce two plausible designs that handle the conflict differently and compare their consequences with affected people.

**Connected patterns:** Many Ways to Flourish (5), Decisions at the Right Scale (8), Begin With a Place (100), Map Who Bears the Cost (101), Small Trials With Real Boundaries (103).

# 103 Small Trials With Real Boundaries

A pilot project is called temporary, but people reorganize their lives around it. Data are collected that cannot be recalled. A commercial relationship becomes difficult to leave. Small scale does not automatically make an experiment harmless or reversible.

A useful trial creates learning while keeping its promises and consequences within responsible limits.

**Therefore, test uncertain proposals through bounded trials with clear purposes, informed participation where relevant, stop conditions, and a credible ending. Protect people from bearing serious risks merely because a project is labeled experimental.**

## A workable form

State the question the trial can answer. Specify duration, participants, resources, authority, and what lies outside its scope. Choose observations that could reveal both benefit and harm.

For the public building, a limited program might test opening hours, staffing needs, accessibility, and patterns of use. Tell participants what is temporary, how feedback will affect decisions, and what support will remain if the program ends.

Compare against a meaningful alternative. A trial that merely demonstrates that some people enjoy a new service may leave the important questions unanswered: who is excluded, what work it requires, and whether another arrangement would serve the purpose better.

Assign responsibility for stopping, adapting, or extending the trial. Include a way for participants and neighbors to raise concerns without jeopardizing their access or standing. Preserve useful learning while respecting privacy and agreed limits on information use.

## Where it fails

Pilots can become a way to avoid ordinary accountability. An experiment affecting essential services or rights requires protections appropriate to those stakes, regardless of its size.

They can also remain permanent pilots, supported by exceptional enthusiasm that cannot be sustained. Examine the conditions needed for ordinary operation before claiming that the trial proves scalability.

## A first move

Write a one-page trial agreement naming the learning question, boundaries, responsibilities, stop conditions, and ending plan. Ask an affected person to identify the promise they would rely on most. Make that promise credible before beginning.

**Connected patterns:** Proportionate Caution (6), The Right to Reconsider (7), Consent as a Daily Practice (13), Capability With Containment (66), Independent Eyes (104).

# 104 Independent Eyes

People close to a project see details outsiders miss. They also have reasons to defend it: effort already invested, relationships at stake, and a hope that the work matters. Independent scrutiny can reveal what commitment makes difficult to notice.

Review is most useful when it is informed enough to understand the project and independent enough to challenge it.

**Therefore, invite qualified scrutiny from people who can examine consequential claims without depending on the project's success. Give them access, a clear remit, and a route for findings to affect decisions.**

## A workable form

Choose reviewers for relevant expertise, practical understanding, and freedom from conflicts that would compromise judgment. Independence is a relationship to the decision, not a claim that anyone has no perspective.

For the public building, reviewers might examine accessibility, finances, maintenance, participation, and effects on neighbors. They speak with users and staff directly, including people who stopped attending or never found a way in.

Agree on the questions while allowing reviewers to identify consequential issues outside the initial brief. Publish methods, limitations, and material findings in an understandable form. Provide a fair opportunity for the project team to respond without granting it control over the conclusions.

Connect review to authority. Someone must decide what happens next, explain that decision, and follow up on required changes. An independent report that no decision-maker needs to acknowledge offers limited protection.

## Where it fails

Review can become a ritual of approval purchased from a familiar provider. Disclose funding, selection, scope, and restrictions on access. Rotate perspectives where useful without sacrificing continuity of knowledge.

Outsiders can also miss local realities or impose inappropriate models. Combine independence with listening, direct observation, and humility about context. The purpose is better judgment, not automatic superiority of distant expertise.

## A first move

Identify the claim your project most needs an outsider to believe. Invite a qualified reviewer to test it, provide the evidence they reasonably need, and agree in advance how an unfavorable finding would change the next decision.

**Connected patterns:** Power Must Explain Itself (46), Show the Work (56), Measure What Matters (63), Auditable Power (65), Small Trials With Real Boundaries (103).

# 105 Publish the Failures

The successful project becomes a case study. The failed version disappears into a drawer. The next community repeats the same mistakes because the public record contains ambition and celebration but little account of what proved difficult.

A civilization learns slowly when every institution edits its memory for reputation.

**Therefore, share useful accounts of failure, limitation, and correction alongside success. Preserve enough context for others to judge what the experience means, while protecting people from unnecessary exposure or blame.**

## A workable form

Describe the purpose, circumstances, choices, observed results, and uncertainty. Distinguish a failed idea from poor implementation, changed conditions, or an assessment that used the wrong criteria. More than one explanation may remain plausible.

Suppose the public building's evening program attracts fewer residents than expected. An honest account might reveal inaccessible transport, unsuitable hours, intimidating registration, or a mistaken assumption about demand. It should not automatically attribute the outcome to community apathy.

Include costs and labor, including work provided informally. A project that appears easy to replicate may depend on an unusually capable volunteer whose contribution is absent from the budget.

Publish what changed in response. Failure becomes useful knowledge when the account helps another person choose, adapt, or avoid an approach. Make it findable through clear names, topics, and connections to the patterns involved.

## Where it fails

Transparency can become spectacle that humiliates participants while leaving institutional responsibility untouched. Focus on causal understanding and fair accountability, with consent and privacy where appropriate.

Failure stories can also be polished into inevitable steps toward eventual success. Preserve unresolved outcomes and genuine uncertainty. Some projects should end, and an honest ending can be valuable knowledge.

## A first move

Write a short account of one attempt that did not work as hoped. Include what you expected, what happened, what you still do not know, and what another practitioner should consider before trying it. Invite someone affected to correct the account before sharing it.

**Connected patterns:** Show the Work (56), Keep Dissent Safe (57), Libraries That Survive (59), Remember the Near Misses (81), Independent Eyes (104).

# 106 Make Better Practices Easier

The responsible choice requires a form, a special request, a higher price, and a long journey. The harmful choice is available by default. Repeated appeals to virtue will struggle against an environment organized in the opposite direction.

Good intentions need practical support.

**Therefore, redesign ordinary conditions so that beneficial practices are accessible, affordable, intelligible, and convenient. Preserve meaningful choice while removing unnecessary friction from doing the right thing.**

## A workable form

Observe where a person encounters difficulty: finding information, obtaining permission, paying costs, arranging time, or maintaining the practice. Ask which obstacles serve a legitimate purpose and which simply reflect inherited arrangements.

In the public building, participation may improve through clear schedules, accessible entrances, dependable staffing, simple booking, and spaces that work without elaborate instructions. These changes support welcome more reliably than repeatedly telling residents that everyone is welcome.

Provide shared resources and usable examples. Repair becomes easier when parts, tools, documentation, and skilled assistance are available. Care becomes easier when support is reliable. Public participation becomes easier when meetings accommodate ordinary lives.

Examine incentives across the whole arrangement. A program asking staff to prioritize quality while rewarding only speed sends contradictory instructions. Align budgets, time, recognition, and accountability with the practice being encouraged.

## Where it fails

Convenience can become covert manipulation when designers decide what is best without explaining their goals or allowing refusal. Make consequential defaults visible and provide a realistic alternative where appropriate.

Making a harmful activity slightly better can also expand its total use. Examine overall consequences rather than celebrating improvement per transaction while ignoring the scale of activity.

## A first move

Follow a willing participant through one beneficial practice from intention to completion. Record every point where they need unusual effort, money, knowledge, or permission. Remove the most consequential unnecessary obstacle and check whether the practice becomes easier for people with fewer resources.

**Connected patterns:** Food Without Cruelty (23), Materials That Can Return (31), Pay for Care (43), Freedom From Machine Persuasion (67), Assemble a Working Language (102).

# 107 Return and Reconsider

The opening ceremony ends, the report is filed, and attention moves elsewhere. Years later, the arrangement still carries the authority of a decision made under conditions that no longer exist.

Stewardship requires returning to see what the work has become.

**Therefore, attach review to consequential commitments from the beginning. Revisit purposes, outcomes, burdens, and assumptions at intervals suited to the stakes, and act on what has changed.**

## A workable form

Set a review date and identify events that should trigger earlier examination. These might include unexpected harm, major cost changes, new evidence, altered conditions, or the exclusion of people the project was meant to serve.

Return to the public building after a season and again after a longer period. Examine use, maintenance, finances, accessibility, and the experiences of neighbors and staff. Ask whether the original purpose remains useful and whether the chosen design still serves it.

Give the review authority and resources. It should be possible to modify arrangements, repair harms, end an activity, or transfer responsibility. A process that can only recommend further study may preserve the appearance of learning while preventing change.

Keep a record of revisions and their reasons. Later stewards need to understand both the original choice and why it changed. This history can prevent repeated swings between options whose limitations were already discovered.

## Where it fails

Constant reconsideration can destabilize commitments and exhaust participants. Distinguish routine monitoring from major review, and give people enough predictability to organize their lives.

Review can also be indefinitely postponed because the project is busy, popular, or politically sensitive. Assign responsibility and make the obligation visible. The reasons for avoiding scrutiny are often reasons it matters.

## A first move

Choose one significant commitment without a review date. Set a proportionate date, identify who should participate, and state which evidence would justify changing or ending the arrangement. Allocate the time and authority needed for the review to have consequences.

**Connected patterns:** The Right to Reconsider (7), Pay for Maintenance First (32), Constitutions That Can Learn (54), Measure What Matters (63), Keep the Language Open (108).

# 108 Keep the Language Open

The final pattern cannot be a lock. If this book succeeds, people will discover circumstances it misunderstands, beings it neglects, and possibilities its authors could not imagine. Some patterns will need new forms. Others may deserve replacement.

A language meant to serve the future must allow the future to answer back.

**Therefore, treat these patterns as a revisable common instrument. Preserve their history, invite evidence and dissent, and keep the process of changing them accessible to people who bear their consequences.**

## A workable form

Give each pattern a stable identity so its applications, criticisms, and revisions can be traced. Record where a version worked, where it failed, and which conditions mattered. A name should make a conversation findable without making its current formulation sacred.

Invite contributions from practitioners, affected communities, researchers, artists, and people whose lives reveal an omission. First-person experience, empirical evidence, ethical argument, and speculation each have a place, provided their differences remain visible.

Let local versions diverge when circumstances warrant it. Preserve enough shared structure for people to compare them, exchange lessons, and understand why a variation emerged. Agreement on every sentence is unnecessary for useful cooperation.

Keep the number 108 as an organizing choice, not a claim about the universe's final inventory of good ideas. A future edition may divide a pattern, combine several, or add one that this book lacks. Changes should improve the language's usefulness and justice rather than preserve its symmetry.

## Where it fails

Openness without stewardship can dissolve into an unsearchable accumulation of assertions. Maintain editorial responsibility, source records, fair review, and clear distinctions between proposals and accepted revisions.

Stewardship can also become ownership of the truth. Make editors answerable, provide routes for challenge, and preserve the ability to develop alternatives. No institution should need this book to become unquestionable in order to survive.

## A first move

Choose the pattern you most doubt. Find a situation that tests it, record what happens, and propose a correction that another person can examine. Begin there. Somewhere, a thousand years from now, someone may inherit the room you left them to disagree.

**Connected patterns:** Leave Room for the Future (1), Many Ways to Flourish (5), Show the Work (56), Keep Dissent Safe (57), Return and Reconsider (107).


# A working kit

## The pattern worksheet

Use one worksheet for a consequential decision, then revise it as the work changes. Keep the answers short enough that participants can read and challenge them.

1. **Place and purpose.** Where are we working, and what should become better for whom?
2. **Current conditions.** What is already happening? What do we know through observation, and what are we assuming?
3. **People and other lives affected.** Who benefits, who bears burdens, and who is missing from the decision? Who represents absent interests, and how are those representatives accountable?
4. **Selected patterns.** Which three to seven patterns matter most? What does each require here?
5. **Tensions and alternatives.** Where do the patterns pull in different directions? What are at least two feasible responses, including the consequences of doing nothing?
6. **Authority and consent.** Who can decide, refuse, appeal, pause, or require a change? Which permissions are needed before beginning?
7. **Resources and care.** Who will do the work, pay the costs, maintain the result, and inherit unfinished duties?
8. **Trial and boundaries.** What can we learn through a limited first step? What would cause us to stop? How will we end responsibly?
9. **Evidence and review.** What would count as improvement or harm? Who will examine the result independently, and when?
10. **Public memory.** What should be shared, what should remain private, and where will the account be kept for successors?

## A first month

**Week one: learn the place.** Observe, listen, and write a short account others can correct. Choose a question small enough to investigate and important enough to matter.

**Week two: compose the patterns.** Select a few relevant chapters, map benefits and burdens, and develop two plausible responses. Find the person or interest most likely to be overlooked.

**Week three: prepare a bounded action.** Establish resources, permissions, responsibilities, a review date, and an ending plan. Begin a small trial only where the stakes and conditions make that appropriate.

**Week four: examine and record.** Look for unintended effects and ask an independent person to challenge the account. Decide whether to continue, change, stop, or gather more evidence. Share what others can use.

# A living research and interview practice

## Sorting material with JEV

Use the 108 pattern numbers as stable addresses for the research. A title may change in a later edition; the address preserves the connection between the pattern, its history, and the material gathered around it.

The proposed JEV workflow begins with a source, not a conclusion. Keep the original item and its provenance, then classify the particular claims or passages relevant to the language. An article may support one pattern, challenge another, and contain background useful to several more.

For each item, preserve these fields:

- **Source identity:** a stable record ID, title, creator, date, original URL or location, and access date where relevant.
- **Relevant passage or claim:** a short quotation where permitted, or a clearly labeled paraphrase with a page, timestamp, or other locator.
- **Pattern relationships:** one primary pattern when useful, additional related patterns, and a brief reason for each relationship.
- **Relationship to the proposal:** supports, challenges, qualifies, illustrates, or supplies context. These describe how the item relates to a pattern; they do not establish its truth.
- **Kind of material:** research, reported case, first-person experience, ethical argument, practical method, historical account, or speculation.
- **Evidence notes:** methods, limitations, conflicts, alternative interpretations, and what remains unverified.
- **Permissions and sensitivity:** attribution, quotation or reuse terms, consent, and any restrictions on circulation.
- **Editorial status:** unreviewed, reviewed, disputed, incorporated, or superseded, with the reviewer and date.
- **Revision connection:** the pattern version or manuscript passage influenced by the item, and the reason for the change.

Allow an item to remain unclassified when no pattern fits. This is useful evidence about the language's boundaries. Do not force every source into a single chapter or treat a high-confidence match as a high-confidence factual claim.

Review a sample of classifications regularly. Look especially for missed contrary evidence, overly broad tags, and passages separated from the conditions that make them meaningful. Preserve disagreement about classification where it matters.

## One hundred and eight conversations

The companion podcast can give each pattern one principal guest and one sustained conversation. Choose guests for a revealing relationship to the problem: someone who has practiced the pattern, lived with its failure, researched its conditions, or developed a serious objection to it.

Across the whole series, seek breadth of geography, age, discipline, social position, and lived experience. A series about civilization should not become 108 versions of the same professional conversation.

The guest is an entry into the pattern, not its sole authority. Research for each episode should include at least one substantial challenge to the chapter's proposal. Where an affected interest cannot speak directly, examine the qualifications and limitations of those representing it.

Use a recurring conversation structure:

1. **The lived scene.** Tell us about a particular moment when this problem became real to you.
2. **The proposed pattern.** Which part of the chapter seems useful, and which part feels wrong or incomplete?
3. **The working example.** What did people actually do, under which conditions, and with what resources?
4. **The burden.** Who paid, who was excluded, and who performed work that the public account overlooks?
5. **The strongest objection.** When would this pattern make things worse?
6. **The evidence.** What supports the conclusion, what remains uncertain, and what would change your mind?
7. **The neighboring pattern.** Which other condition had to be present for this one to work?
8. **The inheritance.** What should a person alive a thousand years from now be able to understand, keep, or refuse about this proposal?
9. **The first move.** What can a listener responsibly try, and what should they observe afterward?

Agree on recording, publication, attribution, quotation, and any sensitive material before the interview. Afterward, preserve a transcript with timestamps, check consequential factual claims, and distinguish the guest's experience from broader conclusions.

Attach the episode to its primary pattern and any meaningful secondary patterns in JEV. Produce an episode note, an evidence note, and a proposed chapter revision where warranted. A conversation that changes nothing may still be valuable; a disagreement should not be edited away to make the book appear complete.

# Evidence, disagreements, and revision

## An evidence register

Keep four kinds of claim distinct when reading or extending the book.

**Observed or researched claims** describe findings about the world. The climate, biodiversity, food-system, and early-development references in this edition are examples. Their methods, dates, scope, and limitations matter. A source about a broad relationship does not automatically validate every local application.

**Ethical commitments** state what ought to matter: dignity, consent, fair burdens, care for other beings, or room for descendants to choose. Evidence can reveal their consequences and tensions, but cannot remove the need to argue for them.

**Design hypotheses** propose that a particular arrangement may serve those commitments under identifiable conditions. Most of the patterns belong here. Test them through practice, comparison, independent review, and attention to who experiences the result differently.

**Speculative scenarios** explore possibilities that are not established descriptions of the present or predictions of the future. The newspaper feature from 3026 and the future-contact scenarios belong here. Their purpose is to make questions concrete enough to examine.

The source guide records selected foundations and points of departure. It is not a systematic review of every field addressed by the book. A high-stakes application needs current, situation-specific inquiry and appropriate expertise.

## Disagreements worth preserving

**Present need and future options.** A reversible choice may cost more or serve urgent needs less well. This language gives present survival and dignity substantial weight while requiring an honest account of what future people will inherit. New evidence about feasibility or irreversible harm can change the balance.

**Local authority and wider obligation.** Local knowledge and participation can improve decisions; local control can also export damage or exclude outsiders. The working question is which decisions belong at which scale and how those scales remain answerable to one another.

**Openness and protection.** Shared knowledge supports scrutiny and learning. Privacy, consent, and specific hazards can justify limits. Restrictions should identify the interest protected, the authority responsible, and the conditions for review.

**Innovation and continuity.** Rapid change can enlarge freedom or destroy capacities people need. Preservation can sustain valuable relationships or entrench domination. Examine the concrete alternatives and distribution of costs rather than treating speed or stability as inherently good.

**Moral concern and uncertain minds.** Resemblance is imperfect evidence of experience, and unfamiliarity is imperfect evidence of its absence. Precaution, research, and revisable protection must be weighed without claiming that consciousness or personhood has a simple agreed test.

These disagreements are part of the language's working structure. Recording a serious objection makes an application more intelligible to its successors.

## Intellectual lineage and contributions

The pattern-language form is indebted to Christopher Alexander, Sara Ishikawa, Murray Silverstein, and their collaborators. The wider concerns of stewardship, care, commons, rights, democratic participation, and intergenerational responsibility have many intellectual and practical lineages. The sources below identify particular works used in this edition; they do not exhaust those histories or imply endorsement by their authors.

For future contributions, record the contributor's preferred attribution, the material offered, permission to use it, affected pattern numbers, and the change made. Preserve substantive dissent alongside an accepted revision. Participation in an interview or review should not be represented as agreement with the whole book.

## Revision record

This edition establishes the full sequence of Patterns 1–108. Each future revision should record the pattern number, version date, previous wording, proposed change, reason, supporting and contrary material, affected cross-references, and decision. Retired formulations should remain traceable where privacy and consent permit.

Stable numbers make the history navigable. Clear reasons make it useful.
