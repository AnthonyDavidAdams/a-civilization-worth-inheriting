# A Fair Transition

The closure announcement describes a necessary change. For the people whose wages, friendships, identity, and local services depend on the old industry, it also describes a rupture. Calling the change inevitable does not decide who should bear its costs.

A civilization must sometimes stop harmful practices. It can do so without treating the people entangled in them as expendable.

**Therefore, plan necessary transitions with affected workers and communities, sharing their costs and creating credible routes to a worthwhile next life. Begin before the old arrangement collapses.**

## A workable form

Identify dependence beyond direct employment. Suppliers, public budgets, family care arrangements, local institutions, and cultural identities may rely on the same activity. A training voucher alone rarely addresses this whole web.

Imagine a town preparing for the retirement of a major industrial facility. Workers help shape the timetable and the assessment of transferable skills. The plan includes income continuity, restoration work, practical training linked to plausible demand, and support for public services during the change.

Make promises specific enough to evaluate. Who is eligible? Where will new work be located? What happens to people unable to relocate or retrain? How will unpaid caregivers and workers on insecure contracts be included?

Provide support without guaranteeing that every existing activity continues indefinitely. Where delay causes serious harm, protection for livelihoods must accompany timely change. The objective is continuity of dignity and opportunity, not preservation of every process or owner's return.

## Where it fails

Transition can become a word for a subsidy with no transition attached. Link support to verifiable changes and distinguish assistance to people from unconditional rescue of incumbent firms.

It can also become consultation after the meaningful decisions have been made. Give affected communities influence over realistic options, including how benefits, burdens, and residual risks are distributed.

## A first move

Choose one foreseeable closure or major technological change. Convene affected workers, residents, service providers, and decision-makers to identify three promises a transition plan must fulfill. Assign responsibility and funding to each before announcing success.
