# No Irreversible Surrender

An institution hands more decisions to a system because each individual transfer appears convenient. Eventually the institution cannot operate, understand its own records, or change direction without that system's cooperation.

Loss of control can emerge through accumulation even when no one consciously chooses it.

**Therefore, avoid transfers of essential authority that eliminate the practical ability to reconsider. Preserve alternative capabilities, intelligible records, exit routes, and legitimate governance over systems on which collective life depends.**

## A workable form

Examine dependence before expanding it. Ask whether the institution can change suppliers, revoke permissions, recover its information, continue essential services, and understand the reasons behind consequential decisions.

Imagine a region adopting an integrated AI service for public administration. It retains accountable decision-makers, usable records, interoperable systems, and a tested continuity plan. Migration and termination are designed into contracts and operations rather than postponed until the relationship fails.

Distinguish technical control from legitimate control. A switch held by an unaccountable company does not preserve public agency. Governance must include the people affected, with authority that can be exercised in practice.

The possibility that an artificial being might deserve moral consideration complicates how it should be treated; it does not automatically justify giving it unrestricted power over others. Questions of standing, delegation, and institutional authority require separate reasons.

## Where it fails

Reversibility can be claimed when restoration would take years of disruption. Test the actual cost, time, and expertise required. Some dependencies can only be managed, not cleanly undone; acknowledge that before entering them.

The pursuit of perfect independence can also prevent useful cooperation. Preserve enough capacity to negotiate and recover, while accepting dependencies that are transparent, bounded, and fairly governed.

## A first move

Choose one essential system and ask what would happen if its provider changed terms or withdrew tomorrow. Conduct a realistic exit review. Address the single dependency that would most seriously undermine the institution's ability to fulfill its duties.
