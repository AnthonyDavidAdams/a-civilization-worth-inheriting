# Return and Reconsider

The opening ceremony ends, the report is filed, and attention moves elsewhere. Years later, the arrangement still carries the authority of a decision made under conditions that no longer exist.

Stewardship requires returning to see what the work has become.

**Therefore, attach review to consequential commitments from the beginning. Revisit purposes, outcomes, burdens, and assumptions at intervals suited to the stakes, and act on what has changed.**

## A workable form

Set a review date and identify events that should trigger earlier examination. These might include unexpected harm, major cost changes, new evidence, altered conditions, or the exclusion of people the project was meant to serve.

Return to the public building after a season and again after a longer period. Examine use, maintenance, finances, accessibility, and the experiences of neighbors and staff. Ask whether the original purpose remains useful and whether the chosen design still serves it.

Give the review authority and resources. It should be possible to modify arrangements, repair harms, end an activity, or transfer responsibility. A process that can only recommend further study may preserve the appearance of learning while preventing change.

Keep a record of revisions and their reasons. Later stewards need to understand both the original choice and why it changed. This history can prevent repeated swings between options whose limitations were already discovered.

## Where it fails

Constant reconsideration can destabilize commitments and exhaust participants. Distinguish routine monitoring from major review, and give people enough predictability to organize their lives.

Review can also be indefinitely postponed because the project is busy, popular, or politically sensitive. Assign responsibility and make the obligation visible. The reasons for avoiding scrutiny are often reasons it matters.

## A first move

Choose one significant commitment without a review date. Set a proportionate date, identify who should participate, and state which evidence would justify changing or ending the arrangement. Allocate the time and authority needed for the review to have consequences.
