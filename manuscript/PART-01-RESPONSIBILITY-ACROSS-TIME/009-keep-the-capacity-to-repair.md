# Keep the Capacity to Repair

A system has not truly been handed on if only its creator can keep it alive. The dependence may be concealed by an excellent service record. It becomes visible when a technician retires, a company closes, or a password disappears with the person who knew it.

Repair includes more than replacing broken parts. Relationships require ways to acknowledge harm. Institutions require ways to correct decisions. Landscapes require knowledge of how to recover after damage. In each case, goodwill is insufficient without practical capacity.

**Therefore, preserve the knowledge, tools, authority, and resources required to restore essential functions and correct harm. Treat repair as part of the original design and an ongoing responsibility.**

## A workable form

Consider a community workshop whose equipment depends on one skilled volunteer. Its first repair project may be organizational. Pay that person to teach others, record essential procedures, and identify parts that cannot readily be obtained. Give a second person access to the information needed to maintain service, with appropriate controls for sensitive records.

Design objects so likely failures can be reached without destroying unrelated components. Maintain a record of modifications. Keep inventories proportionate to the consequences and lead times of failure. In institutions, create equally practical routes for correcting records, compensating people, and changing the procedure that caused a recurring problem.

Ask whether a repair can be performed under realistic conditions. Instructions that require an unavailable tool or an expert who lives far away may describe a possibility rather than a capability. Practice before an emergency reveals the difference.

## Where it fails

Repair can become a reason to preserve something that should be retired. Repeatedly restoring a harmful arrangement may deepen the dependency it creates. Compare repair with replacement, reduction of demand, and a different way of meeting the underlying need.

Nor should affection for repair turn into unpaid obligation. The person who quietly keeps everything working deserves time, recognition, and compensation. A culture that celebrates new projects while ignoring maintenance will eventually exhaust its maintainers.

## A first move

Choose one essential system and ask someone other than its usual caretaker to explain how it would recover from a common failure. Use the gaps to fund training, documentation, parts, or a simpler design.
