# Practice the Emergency

The plan assumes that someone will open the building, someone will contact isolated residents, and someone will know where the equipment is kept. In an exercise, it becomes clear that all three tasks belong to the same unavailable person.

Practice turns imagined readiness into observable capability.

**Therefore, rehearse plausible disruptions with the people who would actually respond and those who would need assistance. Use exercises to discover gaps, improve coordination, and revise plans without punishing honest learning.**

## A workable form

Begin with a bounded scenario grounded in local conditions. Include uncertainty, absent staff, damaged communications, and the needs of people poorly served by standard assumptions. Avoid making the exercise so elaborate that its central lessons disappear.

Imagine a neighborhood and its service providers rehearsing a severe outage. Participants test how information reaches people without reliable internet, how assistance is requested, and how responsibilities transfer when a key person is absent. Qualified authorities guide activities that require technical expertise.

Observe decisions as well as speed. Did participants understand priorities? Could someone challenge an unsafe assumption? Were the people needing help treated with dignity and given useful choices?

Finish with assigned improvements and a date to check them. Record what failed, why it mattered, and who will address it. The exercise is incomplete until learning changes resources, procedures, or relationships.

## Where it fails

Rehearsal can become a performance designed to reassure observers. Announced perfection reveals little about uncertain conditions. Use realistic complications and independent observation without humiliating participants.

Exercises can also cause distress, confusion, or unnecessary danger. Give appropriate notice, define boundaries, provide accessible participation, and allow people to step out. Preparedness should build trust rather than consume it.

## A first move

Run a short tabletop exercise for one plausible interruption. Ask each participant what they would do first and what they would need from others. Choose the three most consequential gaps, assign responsibility, and schedule a repeat after they have been addressed.
